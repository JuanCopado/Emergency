# Trauma and surgical modules

Shared gate: use an organized primary survey, expose while preventing hypothermia,
identify anticoagulation and pregnancy, repeat examinations and involve definitive
surgical/interventional teams early.

## major-burns

- Stop exposure safely, remove constricting jewelry and non-adherent clothing, assess ABCDE and associated trauma, and prevent hypothermia. Use cool running water for initial thermal-burn cooling when feasible; never ice or prolonged whole-body cooling. Cover with clean non-adherent material. Brush dry chemicals off before irrigation when appropriate and identify water-reactive exceptions with toxicology; electrical burns may hide deep injury.
- Estimate depth and percentage total body surface area (TBSA) using a body chart/Lund-Browder assessment. Exclude superficial erythema from resuscitation TBSA. A photograph alone cannot establish depth, circumferential compromise or whole-body TBSA; document uncertainty and reassess.
- Consult a burn center immediately for full-thickness burns, partial-thickness burns at least 10% TBSA, deep burns involving critical sites/joints, suspected inhalation injury, major chemical/high-voltage injury, associated trauma, important comorbidity or uncontrolled pain. These referral criteria do not equal the threshold for formula-based fluid resuscitation. Children require a pediatric burn pathway.
- For adults with at least 20% TBSA burns, the ABA 2024 guideline recommends an initial estimate of 2 mL/kg/%TBSA over the first 24 hours. Use lactated Ringer's/balanced crystalloid and the burn-center protocol. In a modified Brooke schedule, half is allocated to the first 8 hours FROM INJURY and half to the next 16 hours. Subtract fluids already given and account for time elapsed; never give a blind catch-up bolus or restart the clock at admission.
- Example only: 80 kg, 30% TBSA, immediately after injury: 2 x 80 x 30 = 4,800 mL/24 h; 2,400/8 = 300 mL/h initially and 2,400/16 = 150 mL/h subsequently, subject to hourly titration. This is an initial estimate, not a fixed prescription. Do not apply this adult formula alone to children, electrical rhabdomyolysis or small burns.
- Monitor hourly urine output (usual adult target about 0.5 mL/kg/h), perfusion, lactate trend, renal function, electrolytes and cumulative balance. Check catheter patency and other causes of oliguria before escalating fluid. Avoid fluid creep, pulmonary edema and abdominal/extremity compartment syndromes. Consider albumin with the burn team, especially in large burns; timing and dose need specialist selection.
- Shock disproportionate to burn size or very early after injury demands a search for hemorrhage, CO/cyanide toxicity, tension pneumothorax or cardiac injury. Do not attribute all hypotension to burn shock.
- Provide titrated IV analgesia through `sedoanalgesia`/`medication-selection-safety`, tetanus prophylaxis as indicated and appropriate wound care. No routine systemic prophylactic antibiotics for an uncomplicated thermal burn. Circumferential deep burns with impaired perfusion or chest excursion require urgent burn-surgical assessment for escharotomy, not automatic intervention from appearance alone.
- Record time, TBSA estimate, weight, prior fluids, hourly rates/output, serial neurovascular findings and airway trajectory for transfer. Stabilization and early burn-center discussion proceed together.

## smoke-inhalation-toxic-injury

- Separate upper-airway thermal edema, lower-airway chemical injury, carbon monoxide poisoning and possible cyanide toxicity; they can coexist. Obtain enclosure/exposure history, soot, voice change, stridor, work of breathing, consciousness, ECG, blood gas with co-oximetry and lactate. Do not use a normal initial chest radiograph to exclude inhalation injury.
- Give the highest achievable oxygen concentration immediately for suspected CO poisoning, typically reservoir mask at 15 L/min with an inflated reservoir; if intubated, initially use FiO2 1.0. A normal conventional SpO2 or PaO2 does not exclude CO poisoning. Measure COHb by blood co-oximetry; interpret with exposure-to-sampling delay and oxygen already given, not as a severity score in isolation.
- Intubate early with expert airway support for progressive obstruction/edema, stridor, respiratory failure or inability to protect the airway; consider transfer duration and anticipated progression. Singed hairs alone do not mandate intubation. Route to `airway-rsi`, prepare a difficult-airway rescue plan and reassess frequently. Bronchoscopy/laryngoscopy may define injury in a stabilized patient but must not delay a threatened airway.
- For CO exposure, consult toxicology/hyperbaric services for loss of consciousness, neurologic deficit, cardiac injury, severe acidosis or COHb above about 25--30%; clinical severity can justify consultation below that range. Pregnancy warrants urgent specialist consideration at lower exposure severity. Continue oxygen during consultation/transfer and arrange advice about delayed neurologic symptoms.
- Suspect severe cyanide toxicity after enclosed-space smoke with altered consciousness, shock or marked lactate elevation; lactate at least 10 mmol/L supports suspicion in smoke exposure but is nonspecific and is not required before treating a convincing collapse. Do not wait for a cyanide assay. Seek toxicology support without delaying resuscitation.
- Adult hydroxocobalamin: 5 g IV, prepared to a total of 200 mL with sodium chloride 0.9% as specified by the verified presentation (25 mg/mL; either one 5 g vial or two 2.5 g vials with their corresponding reconstitution volumes), over 15 minutes. If a pump is used, 200 mL/0.25 h = 800 mL/h for that 15-minute dose only; this is not a maintenance infusion. A second 5 g may be given according to severity/response over 15 minutes to 2 hours; adult total maximum 10 g. Pediatric dosing needs the separate weight-based product protocol.
- Do not administer hydroxocobalamin routinely to every smoke-exposed patient. Monitor BP and allergy; EMA advises renal-function monitoring through 7 days after administration; anticipate red discoloration, laboratory interference and interference with some dialysis blood-leak detectors. Obtain samples before treatment if this causes no delay. Use a separate compatible line; nitrite-based antidotes can worsen oxygen delivery with concurrent CO poisoning and require toxicologist direction.
- Manage bronchospasm with inhaled bronchodilator when present; provide secretion clearance and lung-protective ventilation if needed. Do not use systemic steroids or antibiotics routinely solely for inhalation injury. Nebulized heparin/mucolytic regimens are burn-center-specific, not universal standards. Admit/transfer suspected significant inhalation injury and reassess for delayed deterioration. After CO poisoning, provide delayed-neurologic warning instructions and arrange clinical/neurologic reassessment at about 2 weeks (CDC).

## trauma-major-hemorrhage

- Scope: adult major traumatic haemorrhage. Activate the local massive-haemorrhage protocol early when critical bleeding is present or strongly suspected; control external bleeding immediately and pursue definitive operative/interventional haemostasis without delay.
- **Portugal reference:** DGS Norma 011/2013, most recently identified by DGS as updated **18/07/2017**, remains the national massive-transfusion reference. It should be treated as a local framework, not as the sole evidence source for 2026 trauma care.
- **Blood-component strategy:** do not hard-code the older DGS pack `4 CE + 4 PFC + 1 CPP` as a universal requirement. The 2023 European trauma guideline supports either (a) fibrinogen concentrate/cryoprecipitate with pRBC, or (b) FFP with pRBC at an FFP:pRBC ratio of at least **1:2**, plus a high platelet:pRBC ratio. Exact packs remain local blood-bank protocol.
- Use early and repeated haemostatic monitoring with PT/INR, Clauss fibrinogen and platelet count and/or a validated viscoelastic method (ROTEM/TEG). Do not delay immediately indicated haemorrhage control or TXA while waiting for viscoelastic results.
- **Tranexamic acid:** in a trauma patient who is bleeding or at risk of significant bleeding, give as soon as possible and within 3 hours of injury: **1 g IV over 10 min, followed by 1 g IV over 8 h**. Do not substitute the inconsistent older DGS text implying 1 mg/kg/h or 1 g every 8 h as the standard CRASH-2 regimen. Do not wait for ROTEM/TEG before giving TXA.
- **Fibrinogen:** treat major bleeding with hypofibrinogenaemia when Clauss fibrinogen is **<=1.5 g/L** or VEM shows functional fibrinogen deficit. The 2023 European guideline suggests an initial **3--4 g fibrinogen concentrate** (or equivalent cryoprecipitate), with repeat dosing guided by VEM/laboratory reassessment. The older DGS 25--50 mg/kg / 2--4 g framework is historical/local context rather than the sole current target.
- **Calcium:** monitor ionised calcium during major trauma/massive transfusion and maintain it in the normal range (approximately **1.1--1.3 mmol/L**). Correct hypocalcaemia promptly; the European guideline prefers calcium chloride for this setting. Remember that **10 mL 10% calcium chloride contains substantially more elemental calcium than 10 mL 10% calcium gluconate**; do not substitute equal volumes.
- **Platelets:** target at least **>50 x 10^9/L** in major bleeding; use a higher target around **>100 x 10^9/L** in ongoing bleeding with traumatic brain injury/CNS involvement, according to current local/neurotrauma protocol and viscoelastic/laboratory findings.
- **Temperature and acid-base:** actively prevent/treat hypothermia and monitor pH/base deficit/lactate. Warm blood products and fluids. Minimize excessive crystalloid because dilution, hyperchloraemic acidosis and hypothermia worsen coagulopathy.
- **rFVIIa:** do **not** use recombinant activated factor VII as first-line therapy. Consider off-label rescue only if major bleeding and traumatic coagulopathy persist despite definitive haemorrhage control, blood products/fibrinogen, antifibrinolytic therapy and correction of hypothermia, hypocalcaemia and severe acidosis. It is not supported for isolated traumatic intracranial haemorrhage.
- Reverse anticoagulation using the dedicated `anticoagulation-reversal` pathway. PCC for VKA/DOAC-related bleeding must follow the agent-specific pathway rather than the historical blanket DGS ROTEM/PCC algorithm.
- Identify chest, abdomen, pelvis, retroperitoneum and long-bone sources with examination, POCUS and imaging appropriate to haemodynamic stability. A negative FAST does not exclude haemorrhage. Apply pelvic stabilization and urgent operative/interventional control when indicated.
- **Local release gate (Portugal/Azores/Horta):** owner protocol confirmation recorded 2026-11-13; the owner confirms an activation protocol and accepts alignment with the current guideline after source review. Local pack contents, blood-bank response times, products and transfer resources remain defined by that protocol; no specific composition, ROTEM/TEG availability or independent blood-bank sign-off is inferred. Use conventional laboratory monitoring when appropriate; do not delay haemostasis or TXA awaiting VEM.

## upper-gi-bleeding

- Primary survey: assess airway protection, shock/ongoing blood loss, cirrhosis/portal hypertension, comorbidity, pregnancy and antithrombotics. Large-bore access, blood group/crossmatch, serial perfusion/lab reassessment and major-hemorrhage support as indicated; do not use a single early hemoglobin to rule out major acute loss. Avoid both delayed blood/source control and indiscriminate crystalloid loading.
- Distinguish suspected nonvariceal from variceal bleeding early; hematochezia with shock can still be a brisk upper source. Use Glasgow–Blatchford (GBS) with clinical judgment; GBS ≤1 can support outpatient care only if stable, no other admission indication and follow-up is reliable. A score never overrides shock, ongoing bleeding or comorbidity.
- Transfusion: in stable adults without cardiovascular disease, a restrictive RBC threshold around Hb 7 g/dL is usual; individualize for ongoing exsanguination, ischemia/cardiovascular disease and the whole clinical picture. ESGE 2026 suggests Hb ≤8 g/dL and post-transfusion ≥10 g/dL for acute UGIB with acute/chronic cardiovascular disease; evidence is low and the target is not a rigid mandate. For major bleeding, hold anticoagulant; reversal is selective for life-threatening or severe ongoing bleeding with plausible residual drug effect. ESGE 2026 considers agent-specific DOAC reversal/PCC in severe ongoing UGIB, while ACG-CAG suggests against routine use (very-low-certainty evidence); use senior GI/hematology/pharmacy input and do not delay endoscopy. For aspirin, distinguish primary from secondary prevention; preserve/restart secondary-prevention aspirin promptly after hemostasis. With DAPT/recent stent, continue aspirin if possible and coordinate P2Y12 interruption/restart with cardiology. Restart anticoagulation after durable control according to thrombotic and rebleeding risks; timing is individualized. See `anticoagulation-reversal`.
- Airway/endoscopy preparation: do not intubate prophylactically in every case. Consider airway protection selectively for active hematemesis, agitation or encephalopathy with inability to protect the airway. Do not routinely place/aspirate a nasogastric tube. In clinically severe or ongoing active bleeding with likely retained blood/clot, give erythromycin 250 mg IV once 30–120 minutes before endoscopy if no contraindication; it improves visualization, not proven survival. If IV erythromycin is unavailable, ESGE 2026 conditionally suggests IV metoclopramide in selected patients (low-quality evidence); assess QT/arrhythmia, interactions and extrapyramidal/serotonergic risks, and do not delay endoscopy. No routine capsule/blood-sensing capsule for acute risk stratification.
- Nonvariceal suspected peptic ulcer: pre-endoscopy IV PPI may be used per local protocol but must not delay endoscopy or resuscitation. Endoscopy within 24 h after presentation and adequate resuscitation; ESGE 2026 does not require routine ultra-urgent ≤6 h or urgent ≤12 h endoscopy in adequately resuscitated stable patients. Continued instability or uncontrolled hemorrhage requires immediate senior GI/anesthesia/IR/surgical coordination.
- At endoscopy, treat peptic-ulcer Forrest Ia/Ib/IIa stigmata. Use an accepted mechanical or thermal modality; dilute epinephrine injection must not be the sole hemostasis method. For IIb adherent clot, ESGE2026 suggests clot removal and endoscopic treatment when the endoscopist can safely manage conversion to active/high-risk bleeding; individualize when that expertise is unavailable; IIc/III generally need no endoscopic hemostasis. For high-risk ulcers after hemostasis (and selected untreated adherent clot), high-dose PPI for 72 h: 80 mg IV loading then 8 mg/h, or a guideline-supported high-dose intermittent IV/oral regimen. If pharmacy verifies the selected PPI's compatibility/stability at 80 mg/100 mL (0.8 mg/mL), 8 mg/h = 10 mL/h; 80 mg/200 mL gives 20 mL/h. Verify loading administration time, diluent and bag replacement interval; never assume a 72-hour bag stability.
- Recurrent peptic-ulcer bleeding: repeat endoscopy and retreat; a cap-mounted/over-the-scope clip is a rescue option. If repeat endoscopic hemostasis fails, transcatheter arterial embolization (TAE); surgery if TAE is unavailable or unsuccessful. Prophylactic TAE is selective for very high rebleeding risk, not routine. Test/treat H. pylori, retest initially negative tests when appropriate (PPI can cause false negatives), and address NSAIDs, antithrombotics, iron deficiency/anemia and follow-up.
- Suspected variceal bleed/cirrhosis: start vasoactive therapy (terlipressin, octreotide or somatostatin) at presentation and continue up to 5 days per specialist/local regimen; give antibiotic prophylaxis promptly (ESGE: ceftriaxone 1 g/day up to 7 days in ACLD, modified for allergy/local resistance). Endoscopy within 12 h after hemodynamic resuscitation; erythromycin 250 mg IV 30–120 min beforehand if safe. Esophageal varices: endoscopic band ligation (EBL). Gastric varices require an experienced team and lesion-specific glue/obliteration or other specialist technique. Identify high-risk candidates for pre-emptive TIPS within 72 h (preferably 24 h; Child–Pugh C ≤13, or Child–Pugh B >7 with active bleeding despite vasoactive therapy, or HVPG >20 mmHg); persistent/recurrent uncontrolled bleed needs urgent rescue TIPS/advanced-center escalation. After control, plan secondary prophylaxis (nonselective beta-blocker plus serial EBL for esophageal varices when appropriate) with hepatology.
- ESGE 2026 peptic-ulcer timing and endoscopic recommendations concern nonvariceal peptic-ulcer hemorrhage; they do not replace the separate variceal timing/source-control pathway. Anticoagulant reversal/restart is agent- and indication-specific; use `anticoagulation-reversal` and specialist input. For lower-source evaluation and suspected small-bowel bleeding after nondiagnostic upper/lower endoscopy use the diagnostic extension in `lower-gi-bleeding`; escalate to gastroenterology/IR as indicated.
- **Baveno VIII2026 focused update:** variceal therapy is distinct from nonvariceal ulcer care. Start vasoactive treatment promptly for suspected variceal bleeding, generally2--5days or until indicated TIPS; selected shorter treatment after durable haemostasis requires specialist selection. Monitor sodium with terlipressin. For refractory oesophageal bleeding, a dedicated covered self-expanding metal stent is preferred over balloon tamponade as a bridge where expertise exists; salvage TIPS needs urgent specialist coordination. GOV2/IGV1 gastric bleeding needs its own endoscopic/EUS and definitive vascular plan, not esophageal banding by default.
- **Restart/reversal reconciliation:** there is no universal restart day. Document the anticoagulant/last dose/renal function, bleed control and thrombotic indication, coordinate GI/hematology/cardiology and select through the accepted reversal pathway. ACG-CAG discourages routine DOAC reversal/PCC; selective rescue in life-threatening bleeding remains a separate decision. For secondary-prevention aspirin, if interrupted, ACG-CAG supports resumption when endoscopic haemostasis is confirmed. Do not infer local availability of endoscopy, TAE or TIPS from GREEN evidence status.

## lower-gi-bleeding

- Assess shock and ongoing hematochezia immediately; establish IV access, crossmatch, serial hemoglobin/coagulation and antithrombotic history. Resuscitate and mobilize gastroenterology, interventional radiology and surgery in parallel. Brisk upper-GI hemorrhage can present as hematochezia, especially with instability; consider the upper-GI pathway.
- In stable adults without cardiovascular disease, use a restrictive transfusion threshold around 7 g/dL. Individualize for cardiovascular ischemia or major ongoing hemorrhage; an initially normal hemoglobin does not exclude acute blood loss and must not delay hemorrhage support.
- For ongoing hemodynamically significant bleeding, prioritize CTA to localize the source. Active extravasation warrants prompt interventional-radiology assessment for angiography/embolization. A negative CTA does not exclude intermittent bleeding; reassess ongoing instability, including an upper source.
- Once stabilized, most hospitalized patients can undergo nonurgent colonoscopy after appropriate preparation; routine colonoscopy within 24 hours has not improved major outcomes. Uncontrolled bleeding needs escalation, not waiting for elective preparation. Surgery is a rescue option after multidisciplinary localization/control assessment.
- Do not use tranexamic acid routinely. PPI/octreotide are not treatments for a proven lower source; use them only for a supported upper/variceal indication. Withhold anticoagulation for major bleeding; reserve agent-specific reversal for life-threatening/ongoing bleeding with likely residual effect after individualized risk review and urgent specialist input. ACG-CAG advises against routine DOAC antidote/PCC, whereas ESGE lower-GI guidance considers reversal for ongoing bleeding or recurrent instability with hematology coordination (low/very-low certainty). Continue secondary-prevention aspirin if possible; if held, restart promptly after hemostasis (ESGE: preferably within 5 days or earlier). With DAPT/recent stent, continue aspirin if possible and agree P2Y12 interruption/restart with cardiology. Restart long-term anticoagulation after control with a drug-specific specialist plan. ESGE 2021 discusses restarting VKA from day 7 in lower-thrombotic-risk patients and earlier resumption with heparin bridging, preferably within 72 h, in selected high-risk VKA patients (very-low certainty). This is not a routine bridge or 72-hour restart instruction for DOACs: their onset is rapid, and ESGE suggests resumption from day 7 with low certainty, individualized to hemostasis and thrombosis risk. See `anticoagulation-reversal`.
- If high-quality upper endoscopy and colonoscopy do not identify the source, assess whether either exam was incomplete or outdated; repeat/second-look endoscopy only when clinically indicated, not routinely. With overt suspected small-bowel bleeding after adequate negative bidirectional endoscopy, capsule endoscopy is the preferred first-line small-bowel test when the patient is stable and there is no known/suspected obstruction or stenosis. Arrange urgently with gastroenterology; ESGE recommends as soon as possible, ideally within 48 h of the overt episode to maximize yield. Do not use capsule instead of resuscitation or acute localization/source control.
- If brisk ongoing bleeding with hemodynamic instability, activate GI/IR and use the acute hemorrhage pathway: catheter angiography/embolization may be required emergently; in a stable patient with active significant bleeding, CTA can localize and guide intervention. Capsule is not the acute test for an unstable patient. If bleeding is intermittent/slower and patient remains stable, capsule timing is coordinated with GI.
- Before capsule, assess retention risk (obstructive symptoms, known Crohn disease/stenosis, prior small-bowel surgery or radiation); choose cross-sectional enterography and/or patency capsule first when appropriate. If capsule is contraindicated or urgent tissue/therapy is needed, specialist-directed device-assisted enteroscopy or imaging is an alternative. A positive capsule should direct device-assisted enteroscopy for confirmation and possible endoscopic treatment.
- After a high-quality negative capsule, conservative follow-up is appropriate if bleeding has stopped and clinical suspicion is low; treat iron deficiency and monitor. Recurrent overt bleeding, persistent iron-deficiency anemia or strong suspicion warrants GI reassessment and further targeted testing (repeat capsule, enterography, enteroscopy or other modality according to presentation); a negative capsule does not exclude a mural mass or all lesions.
- Discharge only after bleeding has settled, stable reassessment and reliable follow-up; a low-risk score supports rather than replaces clinical judgment. Give explicit return instructions for recurrent bleeding, syncope, dyspnea or pain.

## acute-mesenteric-ischemia

- Suspect ischemia with severe pain disproportionate to examination, atrial fibrillation, vascular disease or unexplained deterioration during shock. A normal early lactate or soft abdomen does not exclude it.
- Obtain urgent arterial/venous-phase CTA without oral contrast. Do not delay solely for renal dysfunction when suspicion is high. Activate general surgery, vascular surgery and interventional radiology immediately on strong suspicion, in parallel with CTA rather than waiting for its result; peritonitis requires urgent operative assessment.
- Keep nil by mouth, give titrated analgesia, restore perfusion with titrated fluids and frequent reassessment to avoid overload/abdominal compartment syndrome, correct electrolytes and start broad-spectrum IV antibiotics promptly. Route exact antibiotic doses through `empiric-antibiotics` and current local renal/allergy guidance.
- Distinguish arterial embolism/thrombosis needing urgent revascularization, acute mesenteric venous thrombosis (MVT), and nonocclusive ischemia (NOMI). For acute arterial AMI, start therapeutic IV UFH promptly unless contraindicated, coordinating it with the vascular/surgical team; WSES supports full-dose anticoagulation before the procedure. Peritonitis alone is not a reason to automatically omit a bolus or defer anticoagulation. Active bleeding, HIT or other major contraindications require an immediate individualized decision. Never let anticoagulation or its monitoring delay reperfusion or laparotomy. Do not copy an ACS, PE or thrombolysis regimen automatically.
- Acute MVT: unless a major contraindication exists, start systemic therapeutic anticoagulation promptly. ESVS 2025 recommends UFH or LMWH first line (Class I, level C); WSES 2022 supports continuous UFH infusion (strong, moderate-quality evidence). Without peritonitis, anticoagulation plus supportive care is first-line. With peritonitis, perforation, deterioration or suspected necrosis, urgent operative assessment/source control; anticoagulation does not replace it. If imminent operation, active bleeding concern or severe renal failure makes rapid offset/reversal useful, UFH may be preferred because it is manageable and reversible with protamine; make this decision with the treating team. Worsening despite anticoagulation may prompt specialist consideration of endovascular therapy, not routine thrombolysis.
- MVT aftercare: ESVS recommends 3–6 months of VKA or LMWH (Class I, level B); a DOAC may be considered as an alternative (Class IIb, level C). Consider indefinite therapy for idiopathic MVT or persistent thrombosis risk factors after balancing bleeding risk. These duration recommendations apply to acute MVT, not automatically to arterial AMI or NOMI. Investigate relevant underlying causes/risk factors with the specialist team.
- Neither ESVS 2025 nor WSES 2022 provides a universal UFH loading dose, infusion rate or monitoring nomogram for this emergency pathway. Use the institution's therapeutic weight-based protocol, actual patient weight, baseline bleeding/coagulation context, renal function, platelet/HIT history, planned intervention and local aPTT/anti-Xa monitoring. Obtain baseline CBC/platelets, aPTT/INR, renal function and electrolytes. When the selected UFH nomogram uses aPTT, a common hospital reference is to recheck 4--6 hours after starting or changing the infusion, but the therapeutic target and stable-monitoring schedule must come from that laboratory/nomogram; do not adopt a universal rule of two stable tests. If baseline aPTT is unsuitable, obtain hematology advice on anti-Xa monitoring. Monitor bleeding and platelet/HIT risk. State the preparation, concentration, arithmetic, mL/h and monitoring when enough patient/formulary data exist; otherwise do not invent a pump rate. For NOMI, prioritize correction of low flow and cause, optimizing cardiac output and reducing vasoconstrictors when clinically feasible. In the absence of bleeding, systemic anticoagulation may be considered individually; neither prophylactic nor therapeutic IV UFH is a universal default for every NOMI presentation. Specialist intra-arterial vasodilator with protective heparinization is a separate conditional intervention, not the routine MVT regimen.
- Necrotic bowel requires surgery; consider staged reassessment. Anticoagulation or apparent pain improvement cannot replace reperfusion/source control. Monitor perfusion, abdomen, lactate trend and organ failure in critical care.
- ESVS 2025 recommendation 37 (IIa B): consider endovascular revascularization first for embolic/thrombotic superior mesenteric artery occlusion, according to anatomy and immediate expertise; this must not defer bowel assessment when peritonitis or necrosis is suspected. Recommendation 40 (IIa C) supports considering staged resection without primary reconstruction and second-look laparotomy when resection accompanies revascularization. Recommendation 41 (I C) supports antibiotics but does not specify a superior regimen; use local guidance. In nonocclusive disease, specialist intra-arterial vasodilators under protective heparinization may be considered (recommendation 45, IIb C); this is not a routine systemic vasodilator order.

## acute-abdomen

- Adult initial framework: exclude hemorrhage, perforation, ischemia, obstruction/strangulation, ectopic pregnancy, torsion, sepsis and aortic disease. Shock, syncope, peritonism/rigidity, sudden severe or disproportionate pain, gastrointestinal bleeding with compromise, persistent vomiting/distension or evolving organ dysfunction require urgent reassessment and escalation. Older, immunocompromised or anticoagulated patients may have subtle findings. Children use `pediatric-abdominal-surgical-emergencies`; pregnancy requires the obstetric pathway.
- Assess ABCDE, monitor physiology, obtain IV access and provide titrated analgesia and antiemetic treatment while resuscitating according to perfusion and hemorrhage risk. Keep nil by mouth when obstruction, intervention or surgery is suspected. Activate general/vascular surgery, gynecology or urology early according to the suspected cause, in parallel with investigation; do not wait for all tests before calling for shock or peritonitis. Use `sepsis-shock` and `empiric-antibiotics` for suspected abdominal sepsis and source control, and the hemorrhage pathway for active bleeding.
- Obtain pregnancy testing when pregnancy is possible, and targeted blood count, renal/electrolyte, liver, lipase, urine, blood gas/lactate and other tests according to the differential. Normal early labs or limited ultrasound do not exclude dangerous pathology; specifically, normal lactate does not exclude early mesenteric ischemia. Review prior surgery, anticoagulants, comorbidity, medication and extra-abdominal cardiac/metabolic causes.
- Select imaging by stability, pain site, pregnancy status and diagnostic question with radiology. ACR rates CT abdomen/pelvis with IV contrast usually appropriate for adult nonlocalized pain with fever and no recent surgery; this is not a universal CT prescription. Suspected biliary right-upper-quadrant disease usually starts with ultrasound. Suspected gynecologic pelvic disease generally starts with transabdominal/transvaginal ultrasound with Doppler and pregnancy context. Use selected MRI, often without contrast in pregnancy, when appropriate; a necessary emergency CT is not automatically prohibited by pregnancy.
- Strong mesenteric/vascular suspicion needs the dedicated `acute-mesenteric-ischemia` or `abdominal-aortic-aneurysm` pathway and appropriately timed CT angiography, not a routine low-risk abdominal study. In instability, bedside ultrasound can assist while definitive teams are activated; negative POCUS does not exclude rupture or ischemia. Move to CT only when transport/physiology permits and it will not delay necessary source control.
- Obstruction with suspected strangulation, ischemia or peritonitis requires urgent surgical assessment, not routine conservative waiting. Appendicitis risk assessment, imaging and selection for surgery versus antibiotics follow the WSES 2025 edition with the surgical team; selected uncomplicated appendicitis recommendations must not be generalized to all abdominal emergencies. Route pancreatitis and other established diagnoses to their specific modules.
- Repeat the examination, vital signs and response after analgesia/resuscitation; use observation and reassessment when diagnosis remains uncertain. Persistent severe pain, deteriorating physiology, inability to tolerate intake or unresolved surgical suspicion warrants admission, urgent specialty assessment or transfer rather than reassurance from one normal test.
- Discharge only after stable reassessment, adequate symptom control, an acceptable working diagnosis, appropriate oral intake and no unresolved emergency features. Arrange reliable follow-up and written return instructions for worsening/persistent pain, fever, repeated vomiting, bleeding, syncope or new symptoms. Document diagnostic uncertainty, investigations and the reassessment/return plan; improvement after analgesia alone does not establish benign disease.

## abdominal-aortic-aneurysm

- Suspect symptomatic or ruptured AAA with sudden abdominal, flank or back pain, syncope, hypotension, unexplained shock or limb ischemia, especially in an older patient or known aneurysm. Absence of a palpable mass does not exclude it.
- Activate vascular surgery, anesthesia, theatre/endovascular pathway and massive hemorrhage support immediately. Place two large-bore IV/IO lines, send crossmatch/coagulation/fibrinogen/renal function, warm the patient and blood products, and reverse anticoagulation when indicated.
- Use bedside aortic ultrasound to confirm an aneurysm when immediately available, but do not use POCUS to exclude rupture. Obtain CT angiography for operative planning only when physiology permits and it will not delay hemorrhage control; an unstable patient with a convincing diagnosis needs direct definitive-pathway discussion.
- Use restricted crystalloid and balanced blood-component resuscitation. Follow permissive hypotension while the patient remains conscious and perfusing rather than normalizing BP; set a patient-specific lower bound for coronary/cerebral perfusion and abandon permissive hypotension for traumatic brain injury or other competing indications.
- Provide titrated analgesia without obscuring deterioration. Do not delay repair for central access, invasive monitoring or nonessential tests. Definitive treatment is urgent EVAR or open repair according to anatomy, resources and vascular expertise.
- Reassess mental status, radial pulse/BP, abdominal/back pain, lactate, temperature, calcium, coagulation and distal limb perfusion continuously. Document ceiling-of-care decisions without allowing age alone to deny emergency vascular assessment.

## pancreatitis

- Diagnose from compatible pain plus enzymes and/or imaging; seek biliary disease, alcohol, triglycerides, drugs and alternative surgical causes.
- Use analgesia, antiemetics and moderately aggressive, individualized lactated Ringer's resuscitation with frequent reassessment for overload, renal function and response; avoid fixed large volumes, especially with cardiac/renal disease.
- In mild disease, begin low-fat oral feeding within 24--48 hours as tolerated rather than waiting for enzyme normalization. Use enteral rather than parenteral nutrition when oral intake is not possible.
- Avoid routine prophylactic antibiotics or treatment of sterile necrosis. Arrange urgent ERCP for pancreatitis complicated by cholangitis; do not perform urgent ERCP routinely for all biliary pancreatitis without cholangitis or persistent obstruction.
- Assess organ failure and cholangitis/obstruction requiring urgent intervention. Admit persistent systemic illness, organ dysfunction or inability to tolerate oral intake.

## orthopedic-emergencies

- Identify open fracture, compartment syndrome, neurovascular injury, septic joint, dislocation, threatened skin and spinal injury.
- Document neurovascular status before and after splinting/reduction; provide analgesia and tetanus/antibiotic care when indicated.
- For procedures, verify sedation, reduction technique and rescue plan; obtain post-procedure imaging as appropriate and arrange urgent specialty care for limb threat.
- **Open fracture (NICE NG37):** give IV prophylactic antibiotics immediately in ED if not already administered; cover with saline-soaked/occlusive dressing and obtain urgent orthoplastic review. Do not irrigate long-bone/hindfoot/midfoot open fractures in ED before operative wound excision. Limb ischaemia or severe contamination requires immediate specialist action rather than waiting for a routine theatre slot.
- **Compartment/neurovascular safety:** document serial pain, passive-stretch pain, motor/sensory findings and perfusion, before and after intervention. Preserved pulses do not exclude compartment syndrome; suspected acute compartment syndrome requires urgent surgical assessment. Consider pressure monitoring when the examination is unreliable. Do not let radiography delay reduction of a limb-threatening dislocation/vascular emergency by an appropriately skilled team.
- **Occult injury and discharge:** NICE NG38 supports considering first-line MRI after clinical assessment for suspected scaphoid fracture. Negative initial radiographs do not alone justify discharge without protection/follow-up when suspicion persists. Confirm safe immobilisation, post-reduction neurovascular status and written deterioration/compartment warnings. GREEN covers this reference framework, not every reduction procedure or image classifier.

## acute-musculoskeletal-pain

- Adult initial framework: screen trauma and nontraumatic pain for fracture/dislocation, open injury, infection, compartment syndrome, neurovascular compromise, malignancy and referred visceral/vascular disease. Spinal pain with weakness, saddle sensory change or sphincter dysfunction uses `acute-spinal-cord-compression`/`cauda-equina-conus-emergency`; a threatened limb or systemic illness needs urgent specialty care. Children and pregnancy require their own assessment and medication selection.
- Document mechanism/onset, examination, swelling/deformity, joint movement, function/weight bearing and distal motor, sensory and vascular status. Repeat neurovascular assessment after splinting/reduction and with deterioration. Use `orthopedic-emergencies` and the relevant procedure/sedation pathway for reduction; analgesia does not replace diagnosis or serial examination.
- Suspect compartment syndrome with pain disproportionate to injury, pain on passive stretch or increasing analgesic requirement; normal pulses do not exclude compartment syndrome. Obtain immediate senior orthopedic/surgical review. At-risk patients need documented hourly reassessment; unreliable/equivocal examinations may require pressure measurement by the surgical team. Release constricting circumferential dressings and reassess urgently. Established compartment syndrome requires immediate decompression; do not delay for routine imaging.
- An acutely painful hot/swollen joint with restricted movement may be septic even without fever. Arrange urgent joint aspiration and orthopedic assessment; send bacterial identification, cell count/differential and crystals. Crystals or normal blood inflammatory markers do not exclude infection. In a stable patient obtain diagnostic samples before antibiotics; in sepsis or septic shock start antibiotics promptly without waiting for aspiration and arrange source control. Follow `empiric-antibiotics` and the local resistance/renal/allergy context.
- Choose imaging for the site and clinical suspicion. Use validated Ottawa ankle/foot or knee rules only in eligible patients, not as a rule for every painful limb. A normal initial radiograph does not exclude an occult fracture; persistent focal suspicion needs protection, reassessment and targeted further imaging. NICE NG38 advises considering first-line MRI after thorough examination for suspected scaphoid fracture. Imaging must not defer treatment of neurovascular or compartment emergencies.
- ACP/AAFP 2020 recommends topical NSAIDs, with or without menthol, first line for acute non-low-back musculoskeletal injuries in adults. This is not a recommendation for every atraumatic pain syndrome, spinal pain or pediatric patient. Oral NSAIDs or paracetamol are alternatives after checking age, renal/hepatic function, dehydration, gastrointestinal/bleeding and cardiovascular risk, pregnancy, anticoagulant interactions, allergy and duplicate medicines. Use `medication-selection-safety` and the actual product instructions for dose/formulation; do not infer interchangeability of topical strengths or a universal combined-drug schedule.
- Avoid opioids, including tramadol, as routine first-line treatment for this eligible injury scope. Severe injury or intolerance of first-line options may justify individualized, titrated rescue with reassessment, the shortest suitable course and respiratory/sedation precautions; avoid automatic sedative combinations. Apply suitable protection/support with a mobilization and recovery plan, avoiding unnecessary prolonged immobilization.
- Discharge only after adequate pain control, acceptable neurovascular reassessment, a safe functional/weight-bearing or assisted-mobility plan, no unresolved limb/systemic red flags and reliable follow-up. Explain medicine limits and return urgently for increasing/disproportionate pain, tense swelling, fever, redness, numbness, weakness, coldness/color change or loss of function. Document suspected occult injury and its imaging/review plan rather than labelling every negative radiograph a sprain.
