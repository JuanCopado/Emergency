# Neurologic modules

Shared gate: establish last-known-well/onset, glucose, temperature, oxygenation,
anticoagulants, seizure activity, trauma and baseline function. Perform serial
focused neurologic examinations; protect the airway when needed without obscuring
time-critical diagnosis.

## acute-ischemic-stroke

- Activate the stroke pathway immediately; obtain noncontrast brain imaging and vascular imaging according to local capability without avoidable delay.
- Evaluate disabling deficit, hemorrhage, large-vessel occlusion and mimics. Do not use a low NIHSS alone to dismiss a disabling presentation.
- Verify the current thrombolytic agent, weight-based dose, time/imaging window, blood-pressure requirement and contraindications. Pursue thrombectomy assessment independently of thrombolysis eligibility.
- Monitor airway, glucose, temperature, BP and neurologic change; admit/transfer to a stroke-capable unit.

## spontaneous-intracerebral-hemorrhage

- Treat spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage as a time-critical neurocritical and neurosurgical emergency. Stabilize ABCs, establish onset/last-known-well, repeat GCS/pupils/focal examination, obtain immediate noncontrast CT and repeat imaging for deterioration or hematoma expansion according to the local pathway. Add CTA/CTV or other vascular imaging when age, location, history or imaging pattern suggests an underlying vascular lesion or venous thrombosis.
- Stop antithrombotic drugs and activate agent-specific reversal immediately; do not wait for clinical deterioration. Route to `anticoagulation-reversal`, verify the drug, last dose, renal function and available reversal product, and do not use a normal PT/INR to exclude a clinically relevant factor-Xa inhibitor effect.
- In adults with mild-to-moderate spontaneous ICH presenting with SBP 150--220 mm Hg, begin smooth, sustained lowering early when no contraindication exists, aim near 140 mm Hg and maintain 130--150 mm Hg. Avoid large fluctuations and overshoot: lowering below 130 mm Hg can be harmful. Individualize in very large/severe ICH, impending herniation, suspected malperfusion or immediate surgery; this target must not be transferred to traumatic intracranial hemorrhage.
- If nicardipine is locally compatible, 20 mg to a final volume of 100 mL = 0.2 mg/mL: 5 mg/h = 25 mL/h; increase by 2.5 mg/h = 12.5 mL/h every 5--15 minutes to a usual maximum 15 mg/h = 75 mL/h. The alternative 20 mg/200 mL = 0.1 mg/mL uses 50, 25 and 150 mL/h respectively. Use continuous BP monitoring, state the target and lower bound, and hold/reduce for hypotension, malperfusion or neurologic worsening. Verify formulation, access, contraindications and local protocol.
- If nicardipine is unavailable, route to `iv-antihypertensive-selection`: use labetalol bolus/infusion when heart rate, conduction, bronchospasm and ventricular function permit, or ready-to-use clevidipine when available and not contraindicated. Nimodipine is not a substitute. Choose an agent that minimizes BP variability and can be stopped rapidly; do not delay control while searching for one preferred drug.
- Seek immediate neurosurgical/neurocritical input. Cerebellar ICH with neurologic deterioration, brainstem compression, hydrocephalus or volume above about 15 mL requires urgent operative evaluation for evacuation with or without external ventricular drainage. Use ventricular drainage for clinically significant intraventricular hemorrhage/hydrocephalus with reduced consciousness according to specialist assessment.
- For active herniation or an acute ICP crisis, use hyperosmolar bolus therapy as a bridge to definitive treatment and follow the local neurocritical protocol; prophylactic scheduled hyperosmolar therapy has no established outcome benefit. Do not use corticosteroids to treat elevated ICP. Brief controlled hyperventilation is a rescue bridge only for active herniation.
- Treat clinical or electrographic seizures and obtain EEG when unexplained impaired consciousness or fluctuating status suggests nonconvulsive seizures. Do not give routine antiseizure prophylaxis when no seizure is present.
- Do not give platelet transfusion routinely for aspirin-associated spontaneous ICH when emergency neurosurgery is not planned; it may be harmful. If emergency neurosurgery is planned, make the decision with neurosurgery/hematology and the current antiplatelet-specific protocol.
- Start intermittent pneumatic compression on the day of diagnosis. Consider low-dose UFH/LMWH only after repeat imaging demonstrates stability, commonly 24--48 hours after onset, using the local neurocritical pathway. Perform a swallow screen before oral intake and manage fever, glucose, aspiration and early rehabilitation.

## status-epilepticus

- Treat generalized convulsive status epilepticus for a seizure **lasting 5 minutes or more (>=5 minutes)** or recurrent seizures without recovery. Do not wait for 30 minutes. Stabilize airway/breathing/circulation, check glucose immediately, obtain IV/IO access, correct hypoxia/electrolytes and investigate toxins, infection, pregnancy/eclampsia, structural lesions and medication nonadherence in parallel.
- **First-line benzodiazepine — give a full effective dose promptly, not repeated small fractions.** If IV access and resuscitation facilities are available: lorazepam **0.1 mg/kg IV (max 4 mg)**, may repeat once if seizure persists. Alternative: diazepam **0.15--0.2 mg/kg IV (max 10 mg)**, may repeat once. If no IV access: the AES studied IM midazolam regimen is **10 mg when >40 kg or 5 mg at 13--40 kg**, as a single initial dose; smaller children require their pediatric pathway. Prepare airway support before/while giving benzodiazepines; respiratory depression risk is real but undertreatment of status is also dangerous.
- **Established benzodiazepine-refractory status:** promptly give one adequately dosed IV second-line antiseizure medication. Current ED evidence supports clinical equipoise among:
  - levetiracetam **60 mg/kg IV, max 4,500 mg**;
  - fosphenytoin **20 mg PE/kg IV, max 1,500 mg PE**;
  - sodium valproate **40 mg/kg IV, max 3,000 mg**.
  ESETT infused each of these trial regimens over **10 minutes**; use the verified local preparation/product and monitored infusion protocol, not the trial vial concentration by assumption. Choose according to contraindications, interactions, pregnancy, liver disease, cardiac conduction disease and local availability; do not delay escalation searching for a theoretically superior agent.
- Where fosphenytoin is unavailable and local protocol uses phenytoin, use the local monitored loading pathway; IV phenytoin carries hypotension/arrhythmia risk and requires ECG/BP monitoring and strict infusion-rate control.
- **Important selection boundaries:** avoid valproate where contraindicated (notably significant hepatic disease and pregnancy/people with childbearing potential unless specialist benefit clearly outweighs risk); use caution with fosphenytoin/phenytoin in bradycardia, AV block or major conduction disease. Levetiracetam is often operationally simpler but is not universally superior.
- If convulsive activity stops but consciousness does not recover as expected, suspect **non-convulsive status** and obtain urgent EEG/neurology input rather than assuming a postictal state.
- **Refractory status epilepticus:** after adequate benzodiazepine plus an adequate second-line load, involve ICU/neurology, secure airway as required, start continuous EEG and use an anesthetic-infusion pathway (e.g. midazolam/propofol/other specialist regimen) with hemodynamic and ventilation support. No single anesthetic strategy has definitive superiority.
- Pregnancy/eclampsia, toxin-mediated seizures, alcohol withdrawal and severe metabolic disturbances require the corresponding cause-specific pathway; do not apply this generic algorithm without modification.
- Portugal/Azores: on 2026-11-13 the owner confirms all requested local requirements (drugs/preparations, EEG access/circuit and neurology/ICU escalation) and accepts the pathway. This declaration does not identify brands/concentrations or prove on-site continuous EEG; verify exact product and pump preparation for each administration and use the confirmed EEG/escalation circuit. No new independent pharmacy/neurology signature or prospective validation is inferred.

## altered-consciousness

- **Immediate adult assessment:** stabilize ABCs and check bedside glucose immediately. Treat hypoglycemia promptly through the appropriate protocol; **do not delay glucose for thiamine** in a nutritionally at-risk patient. Assess breathing/oxygenation, perfusion, temperature, AVPU/GCS, pupils and focal signs; protect the cervical spine when trauma is possible.
- **Airway and ventilation:** support ventilation and protect the airway according to reflexes, respiratory failure and trajectory, not an isolated score alone. Use monitoring and early blood gas/capnography when hypoventilation is possible. Record the neurological examination and confounding sedatives when feasible without delaying lifesaving treatment; repeat examinations after interventions.
- **Search reversible and structural causes in parallel:** hypoxia/hypercapnia, glucose/electrolyte disturbance, shock/sepsis, seizure including nonconvulsive status, stroke/hemorrhage, trauma, temperature disorders, renal/hepatic/endocrine disease, toxins and sedative/alcohol withdrawal. Obtain collateral baseline, onset/last-known-well, medication list/last doses, exposure history and anticoagulants. Do not diagnose intoxication or psychiatric illness solely from an initial impression.
- Obtain targeted glucose/CBC/electrolytes/renal-liver tests, ECG, gas/lactate and other tests guided by findings; urine toxicology is not a universal explanation of coma. Treat shock, hypoglycemia, infection, stroke or status through their syndrome pathways while the investigation continues. Consider EEG for unexplained persistent impairment or possible nonconvulsive status and specialist assessment when infection/LP is being considered.
- **Imaging (ACR 2024):** urgent noncontrast head CT is generally appropriate for suspected intracranial pathology/focal deficit; trauma or known structural disease needs its specific pathway. Persistent altered mental status despite treatment of a presumed medical/toxic-metabolic cause also supports noncontrast CT. Do not require routine CT for every fully explained, resolving metabolic episode; select subsequent MRI/vascular imaging by the suspected condition. Deterioration overrides a reassuring earlier investigation.
- **Antidotes are indication-specific:** suspected opioid-related hypoventilation requires airway/ventilation support and naloxone directed at adequate breathing, without delaying resuscitation. Observe for recurrent respiratory depression. **Do not give flumazenil empirically for unexplained coma**: mixed ingestion, chronic benzodiazepine exposure or seizure/arrhythmia risk can make it harmful; reserve it for selected appropriate cases with toxicology/rescue support. Do not use a blanket sedative/antidote cocktail to replace diagnosis.
- **Destination and reassessment:** unexplained/persistent impairment, recurrent events, new deficits, unstable physiology, inadequate airway protection or inability to implement safe treatment requires monitored admission and appropriate neurology/ICU escalation. Full recovery alone is insufficient for discharge without identifying the likely cause, recurrence risk, observation needs and safe supervision/follow-up. Document serial state, response, capacity/safeguarding and handover plan. GREEN validates the reviewed reference scope and owner acceptance, not autonomous diagnosis or guaranteed local resources.

## headache-sah

- Identify thunderclap onset, neurologic deficit, meningism, exertional onset, pregnancy/postpartum state, immunosuppression, cancer and anticoagulation.
- Use a validated SAH pathway and document time from onset to CT. In a neurologically intact patient scanned within 6 hours, a technically adequate modern noncontrast CT interpreted by an appropriately experienced reader may complete the rule-out pathway under current local guidance; do not generalize this to late, poor-quality or clinically discordant studies.
- If meaningful residual risk remains after a negative CT, pursue lumbar puncture and/or CTA according to current guidance, local expertise and shared decision-making; explain the different false-positive/incidental-aneurysm and procedural tradeoffs.
- Nimodipine is used after confirmed aneurysmal SAH to reduce delayed cerebral ischemia; it is not a generic treatment for undifferentiated headache or hypertension and must not delay diagnostic imaging or aneurysm-securing pathways.
- Also consider dissection, venous thrombosis, hypertensive emergency, meningitis, glaucoma and pituitary apoplexy. Escalate abnormal examination, persistent high-risk suspicion or clinical deterioration.

## cns-infection

- Treat suspected bacterial meningitis/encephalitis as time critical; obtain blood cultures promptly, but never allow cultures, lumbar puncture or CT to delay empiric antimicrobials when delay would be unsafe.
- Determine whether imaging is needed before LP from clinical contraindications rather than ordering CT routinely. Give dexamethasone with or just before the first antibiotic dose when bacterial meningitis is suspected and the applicable protocol recommends it; benefit is less certain once antibiotics are already established.
- Add IV acyclovir promptly when encephalitis is plausible, with renal adjustment and hydration/renal monitoring.
- Use age, immune status, pregnancy, renal function and local resistance to select doses. Apply isolation precautions, manage seizures/shock and involve critical care/infectious diseases early.

## traumatic-intracranial-mass-effect

- **2026 TBI characterization:** document serial eye/verbal/motor responses, pupils, loss of consciousness/amnesia, examination confounders, imaging and relevant pre-injury modifiers. CBI-M (Clinical, Biomarkers, Imaging, Modifiers) supplements description; it is not an Emergency-validated CT rule or individual prognosis calculator. Cohort evaluation published 20 October 2026 supports further implementation research, not automatic treatment/withdrawal decisions. A non-testable GCS component is **NT**, not 1 or 0; do not report an artificial summed score. See `references/tbi-biomarkers-cbim-2026.md` for source-specific boundaries.
- **Upstream adult mild-TBI assessment / biomarkers:** this section also covers initial adult head-injury triage; the mass-effect emergency pathway below remains immediate. GFAP/UCH-L1 is an adjunct only in selected patients meeting the exact assay instructions and an accepted laboratory/clinical pathway. The verified i-STAT whole-blood label covers **age >=18, GCS 13--15, venous EDTA whole blood within 24 h**; plasma assays can have a different 12 h window. Do not exchange platform, matrix, units, cutoffs or sample windows.
- **No automatic CT avoidance or discharge:** a negative result cannot cancel urgent CT, clinical deterioration, focal findings, fracture signs, an unreliable examination or the accepted Horta/anticoagulation pathway. A positive, invalid or out-of-window result returns to clinical imaging assessment; it is not a universal CT order solely because 12 h have elapsed. Discharge still requires reassessment, safe supervision and written precautions. No local biomarker platform or CT-avoidance service is certified here.
- **Performance boundary:** the 2026 whole-blood study reported sensitivity and observed NPV of **96.5%**, not a universal NPV >99%. Predictive values depend on prevalence and setting. S100B belongs only to a specifically adopted low-risk/time-limited pathway, not a replacement for NICE/Horta criteria; NfL/tau/NSE do not acquire routine acute CT-rule status from this update.
- Treat traumatic extra-axial hematoma with ventricular/cisternal compression, midline displacement, neurologic decline or herniation signs as an immediate neurosurgical emergency. Hyperosmolar therapy is a bridge to definitive evacuation, not a substitute for it.
- **Hospital da Horta local pathway (TCE protocol supplied 02/10/2026):** begin with ABCDE stabilization, trauma history/comorbidity review and complete examination. Avoid hypoxaemia (local card: SpO2 <94% or PaO2 <60 mmHg), avoid arterial hypotension (local card: SBP <90 mmHg), intubate/sedate/ventilate when GCS <=8 as clinically indicated, and watch for intracranial-hypertension signs. Obtain CBC + coagulation + renal function in moderate/severe TBI and in mild TBI with anticoagulation/coagulopathy or abnormal head CT.
- **Local Horta mild-TBI CT routing:** GCS 15 asymptomatic with no listed risk factor -> discharge pathway with competent adult observation >=24 h and written alarm symptoms. GCS 15 symptomatic without listed risk factors -> head CT; if normal, observe in ED >=6 h and discharge only when asymptomatic/symptoms controlled. GCS 15 with >=1 local intracranial-injury risk factor, or GCS 13--14 -> head CT. Local risk factors shown on the card include epilepsy history, previous neurosurgery, age >65 years (context-dependent), coagulopathy/hypocoagulation, antiplatelet therapy, alcohol/drug use, impaired ability to assess clinically, high-energy mechanism, signs of skull fracture and new focal deficit.
- **Local Horta anticoagulation exception:** for an anticoagulated patient with GCS 15 and a normal initial head CT, the card specifies ED surveillance for >=12 h rather than automatic ward admission.
- **Local Horta moderate/severe pathway:** GCS 9--12 -> head CT and contact the receiving neurosurgical service (HDES is named on the historical card; confirm the current regional destination). GCS <=8 -> head CT and neurosurgical contact; add cervical-spine CT in an unconscious patient or when cervical injury is suspected. Disposition is based on neuroimaging/clinical course and local neurosurgical/ICU/transfer capability.
- **Repeat head CT locally without delay** for symptomatic deterioration, a fall of >=2 GCS points, intracranial-hypertension signs or a new focal neurological deficit. Any concerning deterioration warrants immediate reassessment; do not wait for a two-point fall before escalating.
- **Evidence boundary, owner-accepted reconciliation 13/11/2026:** the historical Horta card remains undated and is more CT-intensive than NICE NG232. The local 6 h/12 h observations are retained as conservative service choices, not universal international minima. ACEP 2023 does not require routine repeat CT or admission/observation solely for anticoagulation when the initial CT and baseline neurologic examination are normal and no other admission criterion exists. Provide written delayed-bleeding precautions and reliable supervision. The supplied adult TBI compendium is supporting material, not a newly authenticated institutional protocol.
- **Blood-pressure boundary:** the local card says avoid SBP <90 mmHg. For severe TBI, Brain Trauma Foundation supports higher age-stratified lower safety bounds: SBP >=100 mmHg for ages 50--69 and >=110 mmHg for ages 15--49 or >70. ACS 2024 provides a practical adult moderate/severe-TBI resuscitation goal of SBP >=110 mmHg (MAP >80 mmHg when ICP is unmonitored), individualized to perfusion and associated injuries. The older 90 mmHg red line is not the treatment goal. These lower safety bounds are not automatic upper BP targets. Aim SpO2 >=94%, PaO2 about 80--100 mmHg and PaCO2 35--45 mmHg unless temporary rescue ventilation is specifically required.
- Activate neurosurgery/trauma and operating-room transfer now for mass effect/herniation. Elevate the head about 30 degrees, keep the neck neutral, avoid hypoxemia and fever, correct glucose extremes, provide monitored analgesia/sedation, obtain serial GCS and pupil examinations, and reverse anticoagulation when indicated. Do not give corticosteroids for traumatic brain injury.
- Do not apply the blood-pressure target for spontaneous intraparenchymal hemorrhage, aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage or ischemic stroke to TBI. Hypertension may be a compensatory response to raised intracranial pressure. First treat pain, agitation, hypoxemia and intracranial hypertension while preventing hypotension.
- If ICP is monitored, individualize cerebral perfusion pressure, commonly within 60--70 mmHg. Treat ICP >22 mmHg when appropriate in severe TBI because values above this threshold are associated with increased mortality.
- **Conditional antihypertensive example, not routine TBI treatment:** if a neurocritical-care specialist judges controlled lowering necessary, use explicit cerebral-perfusion/BP safety limits. For the verified CIMA nicardipine 1 mg/mL label (60975, April 2026), a calculated example is 20 mg made up to a final 200 mL with glucose 5% (20 mL drug + 180 mL diluent = 0.1 mg/mL), in compatible glass/polyethylene. Its normal-adult start is 3--5 mg/h = 30--50 mL/h for 15 min (5 mg/h = 50 mL/h); increase by 0.5--1 mg/h = 5--10 mL/h every 15 min, maximum 15 mg/h = 150 mL/h. Older/organ-impaired patients require the label's lower/slower schedule. Monitor BP/HR at least every 5 min, reduce/stop for hypotension or neurologic decline. This foreign product reference does not establish Portuguese authorization, hospital stock or a locally ready-to-use preparation; verify the dispensed RCM and use immediately after preparation unless Pharmacy validates otherwise.
- For adult clinical herniation or acute ICP crisis, ENLS 6.0 lists 3% sodium chloride 5 mL/kg IV over 5--20 minutes. Show the weight calculation and order it as a bolus volume plus administration time, not as a continuous infusion. It lists 23.4% sodium chloride 30 mL over 10--20 minutes as a concentrated specialist/refractory option; verify the local neurocritical-care protocol because concentrations and repeat-dose rules vary.
- Monitor sodium, chloride, renal function, fluid status and acid-base status; repeat neurologic and pupil examinations immediately after treatment. Avoid severe hypernatremia and hyperchloremia; set repeat-dose/stop thresholds according to local neurocritical-care protocol.
- Mannitol is an effective alternative when hypertonic sodium cannot be used, but hypovolemia, hypotension and renal dysfunction may make it hazardous. Brief controlled hyperventilation is a rescue bridge only for active herniation while definitive treatment is being mobilized, not prophylactic therapy.
- **NICE adult CT comparison (age >=16):** scan within 1 h for initial GCS <=12, GCS <15 at 2 h, suspected open/depressed or basal skull fracture, post-traumatic seizure, focal deficit or more than one vomiting episode. With loss of consciousness/amnesia, age >=65, coagulopathy, dangerous mechanism or retrograde amnesia >30 min triggers CT within 8 h (within 1 h if presenting >8 h after injury). Consider the same 8 h/late-1 h window for anticoagulants or antiplatelets other than aspirin monotherapy even without another criterion. The aspirin exception never negates other CT indications or the accepted local pathway.
- **Surgical triage (BTF):** acute SDH thickness >10 mm or midline shift >5 mm requires urgent evacuation regardless of GCS; smaller SDH with coma plus deterioration/pupillary abnormality also needs immediate neurosurgical decision. EDH volume >30 cm3 requires evacuation. Nonoperative EDH care requires all of: volume <30 cm3, thickness <15 mm, shift <5 mm, GCS >8 and no focal deficit, with serial CT and close observation in a neurosurgical center. Failure of an observation criterion requires urgent specialist reassessment, not an invented standalone surgical threshold. Do not delay contact/transfer while waiting for numeric criteria in a deteriorating patient.
- Consider short antiseizure prophylaxis for selected high-risk moderate/severe TBI with the receiving team; when used, generally limit it to <=7 days unless an actual seizure or another indication requires treatment. Do not extend prophylaxis to prevent late post-traumatic epilepsy or apply it routinely to every mild TBI. Treat an active seizure through the seizure/status pathway.
- **Portuguese consensus comparison (Ferreira et al., 20 July 2026; DOI10.20344/amp.24437):** proposes CCHR plus GFAP/UCH-L1 for selected GCS14--15 patients within12h and different antithrombotic follow-up. This is a national professional consensus, not an authenticated Horta protocol or a DGS biomarker mandate. Its selected intermediate-risk negative-test pathway must not be generalized to every negative assay; preserve current Horta/NICE routing until the exact assay and a coherent clinical/laboratory pathway are adopted. Only the primary abstract/recommendation summary was accessible, not a full algorithm audit.

## acute-spinal-cord-compression

- Treat new weakness, sensory level, gait loss, sphincter dysfunction, saddle symptoms or severe/progressive spinal pain as possible cord/conus/cauda compression. Stabilize ABCs, use spinal-motion restriction when trauma or instability is plausible, document a serial motor/sensory examination including sacral function when relevant, and assess bladder function. Do not delay escalation for a complete examination in a rapidly deteriorating patient.
- Distinguish the likely mechanism because immediate treatment differs: trauma/instability, malignant spinal cord compression, epidural abscess, epidural hematoma, acute disc/degenerative compression or vascular disease. Obtain urgent MRI of the relevant spine; image the whole spine when malignant compression or multifocal disease is plausible. For suspected malignant spinal cord compression, obtain MRI as soon as possible and within 24 hours; progressive deficits, sepsis or suspected hematoma require immediate specialist discussion and must not be held to a routine clock target.
- With neurologic signs or symptoms of malignant spinal cord compression, give dexamethasone 16 mg PO or equivalent parenteral dose as soon as possible, then continue 16 mg/day while awaiting surgery or radiotherapy and taper under specialist direction. Monitor glucose, GI and infection risk. In suspected lymphoma/myeloma without neurologic deficits, discuss steroids with hematology first because they may obscure tissue diagnosis.
- Do not give dexamethasone automatically for traumatic spinal cord injury, disc-related cauda equina syndrome, epidural abscess or epidural hematoma. Do not use high-dose methylprednisolone routinely in acute traumatic spinal cord injury.
- In suspected spinal epidural abscess, obtain blood cultures and urgent MRI; start empiric IV antibiotics immediately when sepsis, hemodynamic instability or neurologic compromise is present and activate spine surgery/source control. A stable patient may have cultures obtained first, but microbiologic sampling must not delay treatment when deterioration is plausible.
- In suspected spinal epidural hematoma, stop and reverse anticoagulation using the agent-specific pathway and obtain immediate spine/neurosurgical assessment for decompression. Do not perform lumbar puncture before excluding a compressive lesion.
- In acute traumatic spinal cord injury, avoid hypotension and hypoxemia. Current guideline suggestions use a MAP lower limit of 75--80 mm Hg while avoiding active augmentation beyond an upper limit of 90--95 mm Hg for 3--7 days; evidence certainty is very low, so individualize with the spinal/critical-care team and evaluate hemorrhagic, obstructive and distributive shock before diagnosing neurogenic shock. Select vasoactive therapy by physiology and local protocol.
- Definitive care is cause-specific decompression/stabilization, oncologic surgery/radiotherapy, drainage or hematoma evacuation. Arrange ICU and transfer to a spinal/neurosurgical center when local definitive capability is unavailable; reassess neurologic level, respiratory mechanics, perfusion and bladder function during transfer.

## cauda-equina-conus-emergency

- Suspect cauda equina or conus compression with new urinary difficulty or retention, altered urinary sensation, saddle/perineal sensory change, fecal dysfunction, sexual dysfunction, bilateral sciatica, bilateral weakness or a severe/progressive neurologic deficit. Absence of a single classic feature does not exclude it.
- Document lower-limb power/reflexes/sensation, perineal sensation and sacral function as clinically appropriate. Measure and record pre-void and post-void residual (PVR) when this does not delay MRI; catheterize retention and record the drained volume. A PVR above 200 mL increases suspicion, but a lower or normal PVR does not exclude cauda equina syndrome in a compatible presentation.
- Request emergency MRI of the lumbosacral spine as soon as possible. The 2026 UK national pathway target is certainly within 4 hours of the radiology request; use the faster local emergency standard when available. Clinical examination, rectal tone and bladder scan cannot safely rule out compression or replace MRI.
- Contact the spinal surgical service urgently when the syndrome is suspected and immediately when MRI confirms compression. Decompress as soon as clinically and operationally feasible according to symptoms and imaging. Do not use a nominal 48-hour window as permission to wait or delay referral.
- Consider massive disc prolapse/stenosis, tumor, epidural abscess, epidural hematoma, conus lesion and vascular causes. Reverse anticoagulation urgently for a compressive hematoma; obtain cultures and start sepsis-directed antibiotics without delay for abscess with systemic illness or neurologic compromise.
- Provide analgesia without obscuring serial neurologic assessment. Do not give dexamethasone routinely for disc-related cauda equina syndrome; if malignant compression is suspected, follow `acute-spinal-cord-compression`. Do not perform lumbar puncture before excluding compression.
