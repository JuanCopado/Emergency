# Infection, allergy and respiratory modules

Shared gate: assess airway, breathing, circulation, immune status, recent healthcare
exposure, cultures, source control, allergies and prior resistant organisms. Do not
delay stabilization for diagnostic certainty.

## sepsis-shock

- Sepsis/shock is an emergency: obtain lactate and blood cultures as soon as possible, ideally before antimicrobials, but never delay antimicrobial treatment, hemodynamic support or source control for sampling.
- Give antimicrobials immediately, ideally within 1 hour, for septic shock or probable/definite sepsis. For possible sepsis without shock, complete a rapid time-limited investigation and give antimicrobials within 3 hours if concern persists; when infection is unlikely, defer with close monitoring rather than treating reflexively.
- For sepsis-induced hypoperfusion or septic shock, consider at least 30 mL/kg IV crystalloid in the first 3 hours, but individualize and reassess after each increment. Use balanced crystalloid, dynamic response measures and signs of congestion; do not continue fluid solely to normalize lactate. In unstable shock, vasopressor and fluid may need to start concurrently.
- Start norepinephrine first-line for persistent or unstable hypotension, including through a short, well-sited, frequently checked peripheral line rather than delaying for central access. Initially target MAP 65 mm Hg; in adults aged 65 years or older, 60--65 mm Hg is a reasonable initial range when clinically appropriate. Verify preparation and show infusion arithmetic.
- Choose empiric antimicrobials from suspected source, severity, MDR risk, allergy, renal/hepatic function and local ecology; de-escalate when data allow. Escalate source control and ICU placement early.

## anaphylaxis

- Diagnose clinically when there is sudden compatible **airway, breathing or circulation compromise**, with or without skin/mucosal findings, after a likely trigger. Skin signs may be absent. Remove/stop the trigger if immediately feasible and call for resuscitation help.
- **Adrenaline (epinephrine) IM is first-line.** Inject into the anterolateral thigh using **1 mg/mL (1:1000)** solution. RCUK/ERC 2025 adult dose: **500 micrograms IM (0.5 mL)**. Repeat after **5 min** if airway/breathing/circulation problems persist.
- Pediatric age-banded IM doses from the RCUK full anaphylaxis guideline (2021, still the latest full guideline on the official current listing; distinct from the 2025 special-circumstances update): >12 y **500 micrograms**; 6--12 y **300 micrograms**; 6 months--6 y **150 micrograms**; <6 months **100--150 micrograms**. Use weight/local pediatric charts when available and independently double-check small-volume doses.
- Position flat with or without leg elevation unless breathing difficulty requires sitting; in pregnancy use left lateral tilt/positioning as appropriate. Give high-flow oxygen and monitoring.
- **Fluids:** give IV isotonic crystalloid early for shock and reassess. RCUK adult reference bolus **500--1000 mL**; children **10 mL/kg**, repeated according to response and overload risk.
- Progressive airway edema requires early expert airway planning. Do not delay IM adrenaline while preparing the airway.
- **Refractory anaphylaxis:** persistent airway/breathing/circulation compromise after **2 appropriate IM adrenaline doses** should trigger the refractory-anaphylaxis pathway and expert **low-dose IV adrenaline infusion** with continuous monitoring. Avoid unmonitored IV adrenaline boluses.
- Antihistamines and corticosteroids are not substitutes for IM adrenaline in life-threatening anaphylaxis. Treat bronchospasm or other persistent features as adjuncts only after adrenaline/resuscitation priorities are addressed.
- **Accepted refractory infusion reference (owner acceptance 2026-11-13):** adrenaline **1 mg made up to a final volume of 100 mL with NaCl 0.9% = 10 micrograms/mL**. Start **0.5--1 mL/kg/h**, titrating to response at the lowest effective rate under experienced critical-care supervision. Calculation: 10 micrograms/mL x 0.5--1 mL/kg/h / 60 = **0.083--0.167 micrograms/kg/min**. Continue IM adrenaline every 5 min until the infusion is running. Prime the dedicated pump line; do not piggy-back or use the arm with a BP cuff. Continuous ECG/SpO2 and frequent BP, with regular cannula/extravasation checks, are mandatory. Product-specific preparation, stability and replacement interval remain subject to verification; acceptance does not certify these details.
- **Observation/discharge (NICE NG258, 2026):** consider **2 h** observation after airway swelling has resolved and breathing, BP and HR have normalized only with a prompt response within 5--10 min to one IM dose administered within 30 min of onset, complete resolution, two in-date auto-injectors with demonstrated use and adequate adult supervision if needed. Observe at least **6 h after all symptoms resolve** if two IM doses were needed or there is previous biphasic anaphylaxis. Observe at least **12 h after all symptoms resolve** for severe reactions requiring >2 doses, severe asthma/respiratory compromise, continuing allergen absorption, out-of-hours presentation, inability to respond to deterioration or difficult emergency access. These are risk-based minima, not automatic discharge deadlines; refractory cases need critical-care assessment.
- Before discharge: provide written recurrence/emergency advice, trigger avoidance, two appropriate adrenaline auto-injectors with training when indicated and specialist allergy referral. The owner accepts this reference pathway on 2026-11-13; no named pharmacy sign-off, local stock audit or independently verified referral availability is inferred. Keep patient-specific checks and blocked automated IV contracts separate from this evidence GREEN status.

## acute-respiratory-failure

- Identify whether the dominant problem is **oxygenation failure, ventilation failure, or both**, and treat the cause in parallel: upper-airway obstruction, asthma/COPD, cardiogenic edema, pneumonia/ARDS, PE, pneumothorax, neuromuscular weakness, obesity-hypoventilation, CNS depression or toxicity.
- Record SpO2 target, respiratory rate/work of breathing, mental state, hemodynamics and an early blood gas when severe hypoxemia, suspected hypercapnia/acidosis or escalating support is present. Integrate lung/cardiac POCUS, ECG and imaging according to the suspected cause.
- **Hypoxemic de-novo respiratory failure:** ATS 2026 recommends HFNO over standard oxygen and conditionally supports NIV/CPAP versus standard oxygen with close monitoring; this does not establish universal HFNO superiority over NIV/CPAP. Without an immediate intubation indication, select support by physiology and local capability, using `high-flow-nasal-oxygen` or `noninvasive-ventilation`. A P/F ratio <=200 mmHg signals substantial hypoxemia but is not an isolated intubation rule; consider FiO2, support, effort and trajectory.
- **Hypercapnic respiratory failure:** bilevel NIV is preferred for an appropriate COPD/acute-on-chronic ventilatory failure with respiratory acidosis when no contraindication or immediate intubation indication exists. Use `noninvasive-ventilation`. ATS 2026 allows HFNO conditionally in selected less severe hypercapnia with mild acidemia (e.g. pH >7.25), only with close monitoring and prompt NIV escalation; do not substitute it for indicated NIV in severe/worsening ventilatory failure.
- **Acute cardiogenic pulmonary edema:** CPAP or bilevel NIV is appropriate in the absence of shock, immediate airway indication or other contraindication; treat the cardiac cause simultaneously.
- Choose HFNO, NIV or intubation based on physiology, contraindications and trajectory. **Do not use noninvasive support as a delaying maneuver** when there is refractory/worsening hypoxemia, severe fatigue, worsening acidosis, shock, inability to protect the airway, rapidly worsening mental state or another immediate airway indication.
- Reassess within minutes of any escalation and repeatedly thereafter: SpO2/FiO2, respiratory rate and effort, accessory-muscle use, synchrony, pH/PaCO2 when relevant, mental state and blood pressure. Serial trend is more important than a single score.
- If intubation is likely, activate the airway/critical-care pathway early and optimize physiology before induction. Disposition must be to a setting capable of continuous monitoring and rapid invasive escalation. ATS 2026 supports HFNO or NIV for preoxygenation rather than standard oxygen when appropriate.
- **Local circuit acceptance (2026-11-13):** the owner accepts support selection, failure assessment and intubation/ICU escalation and requests GREEN. No specific ventilator settings, staffing roster or independently verified on-site resource is inferred.

## acute-severe-asthma

- Treat first while assessing severity. Mark as immediately life-threatening when there is exhaustion, altered consciousness, cyanosis, silent/poorly ventilated chest, bradycardia/hypotension, refractory hypoxemia or impending arrest. PEF at most about 33% of best/predicted supports life-threatening obstruction when the patient can perform it, but inability to perform PEF must not delay treatment or reassure.
- In immediately life-threatening hypoxemia, give rescue oxygen without delay and obtain expert airway help. Once oxygen can be titrated, use the GINA 2026 SpO2 target 92--95% for adults/adolescents and avoid hyperoxia; do not delay oxygen when oximetry is unavailable. Use 88--92% when there is a separate established risk of hypercapnic respiratory failure. Obtain an arterial/venous blood gas for life-threatening features, poor response or suspected ventilatory failure; a normal or rising PaCO2 in a distressed patient is dangerous and may indicate fatigue.
- Use the GINA 2026 acute-care severity branches (Box 9-6): for severe presentation, salbutamol 6--10 puffs of 100 micrograms by pMDI/spacer, one puff at a time, or 5 mg nebulized; for moderate presentation, 4--6 puffs or 2.5 mg nebulized. Repeat if needed up to three cycles 20--30 minutes apart and reassess after each. Refractory/life-threatening disease needs immediate expert escalation; do not apply a routine fixed continuous-nebulization dose from this module. ICU rescue dosing/delivery requires its own verified protocol.
- Add ipratropium with the initial salbutamol cycles in moderate/severe presentations: GINA 2026 Box 9-6 uses 4 puffs of 20 micrograms via spacer or 0.25 mg nebulized, up to three cycles 20--30 minutes apart. Reassess rather than automatically continuing after the initial treatment.
- Give systemic corticosteroid early: prednisolone/prednisone 40--50 mg PO once daily for 5--7 days in most adults; if oral treatment is not possible, use an equivalent IV dose according to formulary. Higher IV doses are not routinely superior. Ensure an inhaled-corticosteroid-containing controller and technique/adherence plan before discharge; never discharge an asthma patient on SABA-only treatment.
- For severe obstruction with inadequate initial response, give magnesium sulfate 2 g IV over 20 minutes. Example: 2 g diluted to 100 mL = 20 mg/mL, run at 300 mL/h for 20 minutes; this is a timed intermittent infusion, not continuous therapy. Check hypotension, renal failure and neuromuscular risk; routine repeated magnesium is not established.
- Do not use sedatives, mucolytics, routine antibiotics, routine epinephrine/terbutaline injection or IV aminophylline. Give IM epinephrine when asthma is part of anaphylaxis or angioedema. NIV is not routine in acute asthma and must not delay intubation. If deterioration, exhaustion, reduced consciousness or refractory gas-exchange failure develops, call ICU/anesthesia early and intubate with an experienced operator using a low respiratory rate, long expiratory time, low external PEEP initially, permissive hypercapnia when safe and monitoring for dynamic hyperinflation/hypotension.
- Reassess every 15--30 minutes during the first hour: speech, accessory-muscle use, respiratory rate, air entry, SpO2, HR/BP, PEF/FEV1 when feasible, potassium, glucose and lactate when beta-agonist exposure is high. Rising lactate may be beta-agonist related and must not automatically trigger more bronchodilator or fluid without reassessing airflow and perfusion.
- Admit persistent severe symptoms, oxygen need, low lung function, prior near-fatal asthma, poor social safety or relapse after initial response. ICU for life-threatening features or escalating support. Discharge only after sustained clinical/lung-function improvement, with controller therapy, written action plan, trigger/technique review and follow-up within days.

## copd-exacerbation

- Confirm acute worsening beyond usual variation while actively seeking mimics/precipitants: pneumonia, heart failure, pulmonary embolism, pneumothorax, ACS/arrhythmia, pleural effusion, medication/sedative effect and metabolic disease. Severe dyspnea, confusion, cyanosis, edema, hemodynamic instability or inability to speak/eat requires monitored resuscitation assessment.
- Give controlled oxygen to SpO2 88--92% pending blood gas when COPD or another hypercapnia risk is present. Obtain an early gas in severe illness or oxygen need and repeat about 30--60 minutes after a material oxygen/support change. Do not withhold oxygen from life-threatening hypoxemia, but titrate promptly because excessive oxygen can worsen hypercapnia/acidosis.
- Start a short-acting beta2-agonist with or without a short-acting anticholinergic. GOLD 2026 advises against routine high SABA doses: one nebulized dose hourly for 2--3 doses, or 1--2 puffs by pMDI/spacer hourly for 2--3 doses, then every 2--4 hours according to response. Verify the specific product dose/concentration and device; do not automatically import the intensive asthma cycle into COPD. Persistent severe distress requires reassessment/escalation rather than unbounded bronchodilator dosing. Use air-driven nebulization when feasible in CO2 retainers and provide controlled supplemental oxygen separately; monitor HR, rhythm, potassium, tremor and lactate.
- Give prednisone/prednisolone 40 mg PO once daily for 5 days; no taper is usually needed for this short course unless the patient is chronically steroid-dependent or at risk of adrenal suppression. If oral therapy is impossible, use an equivalent IV dose and switch to oral promptly. Avoid routine prolonged or high-dose courses.
- Give antibiotics when increased sputum purulence accompanies at least one of increased dyspnea, fever or sputum volume; also consider prior positive exacerbation sputum culture and invasive/noninvasive mechanical ventilation. Usual duration is 5--7 days, with at most 5 days recommended for responding outpatients (GOLD 2026); individualize hospitalized/severe cases. Pneumonia follows the pneumonia pathway rather than this course-length shortcut. Choose agent and dose from local resistance, recent antibiotics/cultures, bronchiectasis/Pseudomonas risk, allergy, renal function and pneumonia severity; load `empiric-antibiotics` rather than inventing a universal regimen.
- Use bilevel NIV early for persistent acute or acute-on-chronic hypercapnic respiratory acidosis (generally pH at most 7.35 with elevated PaCO2) despite initial treatment, unless there is an immediate airway indication or contraindication. Load `noninvasive-ventilation`, define failure criteria and repeat gas at about 1 hour. HFNO may improve comfort/oxygenation but is not a substitute for NIV in hypercapnic acidosis. Intubate for arrest, inability to protect the airway, worsening consciousness, refractory hypoxemia/acidosis, shock, secretion burden or failed NIV when consistent with goals of care.
- Do not use methylxanthines routinely. Treat fluids, diuresis and anticoagulation only for the demonstrated phenotype; provide VTE prophylaxis to hospitalized immobile patients unless contraindicated. Continue/restart appropriate long-acting bronchodilators, inhaler technique, smoking treatment and pulmonary-rehabilitation/follow-up planning after stabilization.
- Reassess respiratory rate/effort, mental state, SpO2, gas/pH/PaCO2, hemodynamics, secretion clearance and NIV tolerance frequently. Admit for acidosis/hypercapnia, new oxygen or ventilatory support, serious comorbidity, inadequate home support or failure of ED therapy; higher-acuity placement for severe acidosis, altered consciousness, shock or escalating support.

## life-threatening-hemoptysis

- Define life-threatening hemoptysis by airway obstruction, impaired gas exchange, hemodynamic instability, rapid ongoing bleeding or limited cardiopulmonary reserve, not by a single volume threshold. Confirm blood is from the lower respiratory tract while simultaneously excluding upper-airway and gastrointestinal bleeding; do not delay resuscitation for source certainty.
- Activate anesthesia/airway, respiratory/interventional bronchoscopy, interventional radiology and thoracic surgery early. Sit upright if the bleeding side is unknown and airway control is adequate; once the side is known, place the bleeding lung dependent to protect the better lung. Give high-concentration oxygen for critical hypoxemia, two large-bore IV lines, continuous monitoring, full blood count, coagulation/fibrinogen, renal function, blood gas/lactate and crossmatched blood.
- Stop and reverse clinically important anticoagulant/antiplatelet effect according to agent, timing, renal function and bleeding severity by loading `anticoagulation-reversal`; correct severe thrombocytopenia, hypofibrinogenemia and hypothermia. Do not delay airway/source control while awaiting laboratory normalization.
- Secure the airway early when clearance is failing, oxygenation is deteriorating, bleeding is brisk or transport/procedure is unsafe. Use the most experienced operator, suction immediately available and a large single-lumen endotracheal tube (commonly at least 8.0--8.5 mm internal diameter) to permit therapeutic bronchoscopy. Do not default to a double-lumen tube because narrow lumens obstruct with clot and impede bronchoscopy. Protect the non-bleeding lung: expert bronchoscopic selective mainstem intubation must ventilate the non-bleeding lung; alternatively, occlude the bleeding bronchus with a bronchial blocker when expertise/equipment permit. Confirm which lung is ventilated.
- Use rigid or large-channel flexible bronchoscopy to suction clot, localize bleeding and apply temporary endobronchial control when unstable or the airway is threatened. If stabilized, obtain multidetector CT angiography before definitive embolization to identify bronchial, non-bronchial systemic or pulmonary arterial sources; do not send an unstable patient away from resuscitation solely for imaging.
- Arrange urgent bronchial/non-bronchial systemic artery embolization after stabilization for life-threatening or recurrent hemoptysis when a culprit vessel is present; it is usually first-line definitive control for systemic arterial bleeding. For a pulmonary-artery source, arrange urgent lesion-specific interventional-radiology and surgical review; pulmonary artery embolization may be appropriate, not automatic surgery solely because of arterial origin. Surgery is selected for an injury/lesion requiring repair or resection, failed/unavailable embolization or recurrent bleeding requiring definitive resection.
- Nebulized tranexamic acid may be considered only as an adjunct/bridge in selected non-catastrophic or temporarily controlled bleeding; a commonly studied regimen is 500 mg in 5 mL nebulized every 8 hours. Evidence in actively life-threatening hemoptysis is insufficient, and it must never delay bronchoscopy, embolization or surgery. Verify formulation, thrombosis/seizure risk and local protocol; do not nebulize blindly through an obstructed airway.
- Reassess airway patency, suctioned/estimated bleeding rate, oxygenation/ventilation, hemodynamics, hemoglobin/coagulation and recurrence continuously. After apparent control, manage the cause (bronchiectasis, TB/infection, malignancy, aspergillosis, vasculitis, pulmonary embolic/vascular lesion) and admit to critical care or a monitored procedural center; early rebleeding requires renewed CTA/bronchoscopy and embolization/surgery review.
- **Local circuit acceptance (2026-11-13):** the owner requests GREEN for the airway, bronchoscopy, CTA and embolization/surgery pathway after source review. This records circuit acceptance, not independently audited on-site bronchoscopy/IR staffing or a new specialist signature; activate the accepted referral/transfer route when needed.

## oxygen-therapy-emergency

- Prescribe oxygen to a saturation target, not merely a flow. Use SpO2 94--98% for most acutely ill adults and 88--92% pending blood gas results when COPD or another risk of hypercapnic respiratory failure is present. In cardiac arrest/peri-arrest or immediately life-threatening hypoxemia, give the highest available concentration first and titrate promptly once reliable monitoring is available. Do not give routine oxygen to a normoxemic ACS or stroke patient.
- Treat the following low-flow FiO2 values as rough bedside estimates, not measurements. Actual FiO2 changes with inspiratory demand, respiratory rate, tidal volume, mouth breathing, mask seal and equipment:

| Device | Adult flow | Approximate FiO2 | Operational point |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Standard nasal cannula | 1--6 L/min | about 24--44%; rough rule 24% at 1 L/min then +4 percentage points per additional L/min | Comfortable; permits speech/eating; unreliable FiO2 in tachypnea or high inspiratory demand; dryness/epistaxis possible. |
| Simple face mask | 5--10 L/min | about 35--60% | Never run below 5 L/min because of CO2 rebreathing; variable FiO2, poor tolerance/seal and aspiration risk. |
| Reservoir/non-rebreather mask | 15 L/min initially | high concentration, often about 60--95% with good seal | Pre-fill reservoir and keep it from collapsing during inspiration; bridge for critical illness, not a reason to delay HFNO/NIV/intubation. |
| Venturi mask | Use flow printed on the valve/device | fixed labelled FiO2, commonly 24%, 28%, 35%, 40% or 60% | Prefer when controlled FiO2 matters. Valve colours and required flows can vary by manufacturer; never prescribe by colour alone. Increasing source flow above the stated minimum increases total gas flow, not the labelled FiO2. |
| Heated high-flow nasal oxygen | commonly 20--60 L/min in adults | FiO2 set independently from 0.21 to 1.0 | Heated humidification, better matching of inspiratory demand, dead-space washout and modest variable airway pressure; define failure criteria and do not delay intubation. |

- For a common Venturi system only, typical minimum source flows are 24% at 2--4 L/min, 28% at 4--6 L/min, 35% at 8--12 L/min, 40% at 10--15 L/min and 60% at 12--15 L/min. Verify the exact valve label/package and increase total flow when respiratory demand exceeds device output; changing flow does not convert one labelled valve to another FiO2.
- Reassess within minutes after every device/flow change: SpO2 waveform and target, respiratory rate and effort, mental state, hemodynamics, mask/cannula position, reservoir inflation and oxygen supply. Obtain an early blood gas when severe hypoxemia, suspected hypercapnia, reduced consciousness or escalating support is present; repeat within 30--60 minutes after oxygen changes in patients at risk of CO2 retention or sooner if deteriorating.
- Escalate support rather than repeatedly increasing an inadequate low-flow device. Consider HFNO for hypoxemic failure, NIV for an appropriate ventilatory/cardiogenic indication, and intubation for refractory hypoxemia, exhaustion, worsening consciousness, shock, inability to protect the airway or failed noninvasive support.
- Balance benefit against harm: oxygen reverses hypoxemia but excessive exposure can worsen hypercapnia in susceptible patients, cause absorption atelectasis and oxidative lung injury, and mask deterioration. Interfaces can cause dryness, pressure injury, claustrophobia and aspiration risk. Oxygen supports combustion: prohibit smoking/flames and keep oils/grease away from equipment.
- Record target range, device, flow or set FiO2, SpO2 response and escalation plan. Flow in L/min is not interchangeable with FiO2, and FiO2 from low-flow devices must not be used as an exact denominator for P/F calculations.

## high-flow-nasal-oxygen

- Use heated humidified high-flow nasal oxygen primarily for acute hypoxemic respiratory failure when conventional oxygen is inadequate and no immediate indication for intubation exists. HFNO does not correct severe ventilatory failure reliably and must not replace bilevel NIV for a COPD exacerbation with acute respiratory acidosis when NIV is appropriate.
- In adults, select an interface sized to avoid complete nare occlusion, commonly start flow around 40--60 L/min according to inspiratory demand and tolerance, and set FiO2 independently to the syndrome-specific SpO2 target. Optimize the cause, positioning, secretion clearance and comfort; do not treat the device as definitive therapy.
- Calculate ROX only from a reliable SpO2, set HFNO FiO2 as a fraction, and simultaneous respiratory rate: `ROX = (SpO2 / FiO2) / respiratory rate`. Use `scripts/respiratory_support.py` for serial calculations. Record baseline and repeat early and at approximately 2, 6 and 12 hours when therapy continues, but reassess clinically within minutes after initiation or any deterioration.
- Treat published ROX cutoffs as context-limited signals from adults with pneumonia, not universal intubation thresholds. In the original validation cohort, ROX at least 4.88 at 2, 6 or 12 hours was associated with lower intubation risk; ROX below 2.85 at 2 hours, 3.47 at 6 hours or 3.85 at 12 hours signaled higher failure risk. The direction of change and the bedside examination matter more than a single borderline value.
- Define failure before starting. Escalate immediately for refractory or worsening hypoxemia, increasing respiratory effort or fatigue, shock, worsening mental state, inability to protect the airway, dangerous acidosis/hypercapnia, or another immediate airway indication. A reassuring ROX must never overrule these findings or delay intubation.
- Monitor continuous SpO2 and frequent respiratory rate, work of breathing, mental state and hemodynamics; obtain blood gas and imaging/POCUS according to physiology. Document flow, FiO2, target, ROX trend, response, ceiling of care and named escalation plan.

## noninvasive-ventilation

- Match NIV to a **supported syndrome**. ERS/ATS recommends bilevel NIV for COPD exacerbation with acute or acute-on-chronic respiratory acidosis (pH <=7.35 with elevated PaCO2 despite initial medical therapy). CPAP or bilevel NIV is supported in acute cardiogenic pulmonary edema when shock or an immediate airway indication is absent.
- **ATS 2026 update:** in de-novo acute hypoxemic respiratory failure, HFNO is strongly recommended over conventional oxygen; NIV/CPAP is conditionally recommended over conventional oxygen. This does not establish universal HFNO superiority over NIV/CPAP. Select support by syndrome, severity, expertise and response; monitor closely for delayed intubation. Hypercapnic failure strongly favors NIV; HFNO is only a conditional alternative in less severe hypercapnia/mild acidemia, with close monitoring and prompt NIV escalation. Do not treat the older ERS 2022 comparator as a universal preference across all phenotypes.
- NIV is not routine treatment for acute asthma. Before a trial, document reversibility, escalation/ceiling plan and contraindications. Do not use NIV for respiratory arrest, inability to protect the airway, uncontrolled vomiting, fixed upper-airway obstruction, undrained pneumothorax, severe facial injury preventing a seal, or rapidly worsening shock.
- Agitation, heavy secretions, reduced consciousness and severe acidosis increase failure risk and require senior critical-care assessment; a low pH alone is neither an automatic exclusion nor permission for an unsupervised trial.
- **Starting reference, acute hypercapnia:** IPAP 10--15 cmH2O and EPAP 4 cmH2O, then increase effective pressure support over the first 10--30 min according to ventilation, work of breathing, leak, synchrony and tolerance; pressures of 20--30 cmH2O may be needed under an experienced team. Set S/T backup rate individually using the selected ventilator protocol, respiratory drive and patient synchrony; no universal device-independent backup rate is assumed.
- If PaCO2 remains high with inadequate ventilation, correct leak/synchrony before increasing IPAP. For hypoxemia, reassess diagnosis/interface and titrate FiO2 and/or EPAP while retaining sufficient pressure support. Avoid an escalating tidal volume/work-of-breathing pattern that delays intubation.
- For acute cardiogenic pulmonary edema, a common CPAP starting reference is **5--10 cmH2O**; choose bilevel when ventilation assistance is also needed. These ranges require patient-specific reassessment.
- **Interface/circuit:** select an appropriately sized oronasal/full-face interface for acute support as tolerated, protect pressure areas and reassess leaks, comfort and secretions. Confirm the device's circuit, intentional leak/exhalation port or expiratory valve and vented/nonvented mask match; never obstruct the exhalation route. Helmet and other interfaces need their own validated settings and trained staff; do not copy mask settings blindly.
- Target SpO2 **88--92%** in acute hypercapnic failure unless an individualized target is documented. Initially use continuous SpO2/ECG and frequent BP, respiratory-rate, consciousness and work-of-breathing assessment. Repeat blood gas at approximately **1 hour**, after material changes and immediately if deterioration occurs; improvement must include clinical effort and gas trends.
- Falling pH, non-improving PaCO2/work of breathing, refractory hypoxemia, new confusion, intolerance or hemodynamic decline are failure signals. pH <7.25 despite optimized NIV requires immediate senior/ICU reassessment and preparation for intubation when appropriate; no single number replaces the airway decision. Do not await the scheduled gas or prolong NIV when immediate intubation is indicated.
- Record device/interface, IPAP/EPAP or CPAP, backup rate, FiO2, oxygen target, response trend, ceiling of care and explicit intubation criteria. Owner accepts this reviewed framework; model-specific setup and competent staffing remain bedside checks rather than assumed equipment availability.

## tension-pneumothorax

- Suspect tension physiology when acute dyspnea or pleuritic chest pain is accompanied by shock, severe respiratory compromise or rapid deterioration, especially with asymmetric or absent breath sounds. A relatively normal heart rate, absent tracheal deviation or absent neck-vein distension does not establish stability or exclude the diagnosis.
- Treat this as a clinical time-critical diagnosis. Activate resuscitation support, provide monitored oxygen appropriate to hypoxemia, obtain IV/IO access and prepare immediate pleural decompression. Do not send an unstable patient for CT, and do not wait for chest radiography when the clinical probability of tension pneumothorax is high.
- Use lung POCUS only when it is immediately available and does not delay decompression. Absent sliding and B-lines with a compatible pattern support pneumothorax but are not specific; a lung point supports the diagnosis when seen but may be absent in a very large pneumothorax. A technically limited or negative scan does not overrule worsening obstructive-shock physiology.
- Select needle/catheter decompression or open/finger thoracostomy according to patient context, clinician skill, equipment and current local resuscitation protocol. State the intended anatomical site and device before acting, avoid the inferior rib neurovascular bundle, and have an immediate failure/rescue plan. Do not present one technique or site as universally preferred across spontaneous, traumatic and peri-arrest settings.
- Decompression is not the endpoint: reassess blood pressure, oxygenation, work of breathing and bilateral air entry immediately; confirm pleural access/success by clinical response and available imaging, then arrange definitive intercostal drainage and specialist/critical-care involvement. If there is no prompt improvement, check device failure or malposition and reassess for massive PE, tamponade, hemorrhage, dynamic hyperinflation and other causes of shock.
- Use cautious, reassessed circulatory support only as a bridge to source control. Fluids or vasopressors must not delay decompression or be treated as definitive therapy for obstructive shock.

## dermatology-severe

- Look for mucosal involvement, skin pain, blistering, purpura, necrosis, systemic toxicity, immunosuppression and drug timing.
- Threats include SJS/TEN, DRESS, necrotizing infection, meningococcemia, purpura fulminans and severe drug reaction.
- Stop suspected culprit drugs, stabilize temperature/fluids/analgesia, avoid traumatic skin handling and obtain early dermatology/burn/critical-care input. Verify syndrome-specific steroids or immunomodulators before use.
- **SJS/TEN:** stop suspected culprit/nonessential drugs immediately, record exposure timing and obtain urgent dermatology plus early ophthalmology review. Assess airway/mucosal involvement, fluid/electrolytes, temperature, nutrition, pain and organ dysfunction; arrange specialist-unit care when required. Skin biopsy supports diagnosis but must not delay stabilization. Use careful skin handling and organ-specific wound/ocular care.
- **Immunomodulators:** S3 epidermal-necrolysis guidance2024 and the Japanese2025 supplement use differing conditional/systemic strategies. Neither establishes one universal steroid/IVIG/biologic prescription for every blistering rash; document syndrome/severity and use dermatology-led treatment. Do not delay emergency antibiotics/surgery for suspected necrotizing infection while considering a drug eruption.
- **DRESS:** assess eosinophils, liver/renal function and other organ involvement; adult international consensus supports severity-stratified topical versus systemic corticosteroids, with systemic therapy for severe organ disease and a supervised taper. Do not transfer adult regimens automatically to children. Arrange delayed-organ/thyroid and medication-allergy follow-up; avoid re-exposure to the culprit drug. A score or photograph does not alone establish the diagnosis.

## soft-tissue-infections

- Distinguish uncomplicated cellulitis/abscess from necrotizing infection, deep-space infection, pyomyositis and device-related infection.
- Pain out of proportion, toxicity, bullae, crepitus, anesthesia or rapid progression requires immediate surgical consultation; imaging must not delay exploration when suspicion is high.
- Drain drainable collections and select antibiotics by severity, anatomy, host factors and local resistance. Mark progression, reassess perfusion and admit systemic, high-risk or function-threatening disease.

## emergency-infectious-diseases

- Adult initial framework: define the likely syndrome/source, immune status, pregnancy, travel/exposures, recent healthcare/antibiotics, prior resistant organisms and organ dysfunction. Assess ABCDE and perfusion promptly; absence of fever does not exclude serious infection. Children, neutropenic/transplant patients and pregnancy need their specific pathways.
- Apply standard precautions to every patient and add contact, droplet or airborne precautions according to the suspected syndrome/pathogen at triage, without waiting for confirmatory testing. Coordinate isolation, respiratory protection and exposure management with infection prevention/public health; communicate required precautions during transport and handover. A negative early or inappropriate test does not by itself end a necessary isolation pathway.
- Obtain blood/source cultures and targeted microbiology promptly when they will guide care, before antibiotics if this causes no clinically important delay. Route sepsis/shock to `sepsis-shock`, CNS infection to `cns-infection`, and neutropenic illness to `hematology-oncology`; treat time-critical infection and arrange source control without waiting for complete diagnostic certainty. Assess severe malaria urgently in a compatible travel/exposure context with appropriate specialist testing/treatment rather than routine fever follow-up.
- Choose initial therapy through `empiric-antibiotics` using syndrome severity, allergy, organ function, prior microbiology, exposure and local resistance patterns. Do not wait for the current antibiogram to treat an emergency, but local resistance data can change the initial empirical choice. Obtain infectious-disease/microbiology advice for unusual pathogens, immunosuppression, resistant infection or diagnostic uncertainty; no universal antibiotic regimen fits this whole module.
- Reassess clinical response and diagnostic alternatives, review culture results and narrow/stop unnecessary agents when appropriate. Normalizing one biomarker is not a substitute for perfusion, source control and organ-function assessment. Avoid treating colonization or asymptomatic bacteriuria as the sole explanation for nonspecific symptoms; preserve immediate sepsis care when clinically indicated.
- For Portugal, consult the current DGS SINAVE notifiable-disease list/case definitions and coordinate with the relevant local/regional public-health authority. Notify suspected or confirmed conditions as required by that disease's current rules; do not invent one notification deadline or assume mainland response arrangements apply unchanged to the Azores. Notifications must not delay stabilization.
- Admit/escalate organ dysfunction, unsafe oral intake, unresolved high-risk infection or unreliable monitoring/support. Document the working diagnosis, treatment/review timing, pending tests, isolation advice and explicit return precautions for any outpatient plan. GREEN covers this framework, not validation of every pathogen-specific regimen or local laboratory/public-health capability.
