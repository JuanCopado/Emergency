# Infection, allergy and respiratory modules

Shared gate: assess airway, breathing, circulation, immune status, recent healthcare
exposure, cultures, source control, allergies and prior resistant organisms. Do not
delay stabilization for diagnostic certainty.

## sepsis-shock

- Sepsis/shock is an emergency: obtain lactate and blood cultures as soon as possible, ideally before antimicrobials, but never delay antimicrobial treatment, hemodynamic support or source control for sampling.
- Give antimicrobials immediately, ideally within 1 hour, for septic shock or probable/definite sepsis. For possible sepsis without shock, complete a rapid time-limited investigation and give antimicrobials within 3 hours if concern persists; when infection is unlikely, defer with close monitoring rather than treating reflexively.
- For sepsis-induced hypoperfusion or septic shock, consider at least 30 mL/kg IV crystalloid in the first 3 hours, but individualize and reassess after each increment. Use balanced crystalloid, dynamic response measures and signs of congestion; do not continue fluid solely to normalize lactate. In unstable shock, vasopressor and fluid may need to start concurrently.
- Start norepinephrine first-line for persistent or unstable hypotension, including through a short, well-sited, frequently checked peripheral line rather than delaying for central access. Initially target MAP 65 mm Hg; in adults aged 65 years or older, 60--65 mm Hg is a reasonable initial range when clinically appropriate. Verify preparation and show infusion arithmetic.
- Choose empiric antimicrobials from suspected source, severity, MDR risk, allergy, renal/hepatic function and local ecology; de-escalate when data allow. Escalate source control and ICU placement early.

## anaphylaxis

- Diagnose clinically when compatible airway, breathing, circulation or multi-system features follow likely exposure; skin findings may be absent.
- Give IM epinephrine promptly in the anterolateral thigh using verified age/weight dosing; repeat according to response. Position appropriately, give oxygen and IV crystalloid for shock.
- Prepare advanced airway early for progressive edema. Refractory shock needs expert IV epinephrine infusion with continuous monitoring; avoid unmonitored IV bolus epinephrine.
- Observe according to severity, treatment requirements and biphasic-risk factors; discharge with trigger plan, autoinjector access/training and referral when appropriate.

## acute-respiratory-failure

- Identify oxygenation versus ventilation failure and immediate causes: obstruction, asthma/COPD, edema, pneumonia, PE, pneumothorax, neuromuscular disease or toxicity.
- Titrate oxygen to the patient's condition, obtain blood gas when useful, and integrate lung/cardiac POCUS, ECG and imaging.
- Choose HFNO, NIV or intubation based on physiology, contraindications and trajectory; define failure criteria before noninvasive support and do not delay intubation in deterioration.
- Reassess work of breathing, mental state, gas exchange and hemodynamics continuously; disposition to a setting capable of the chosen support.

## acute-severe-asthma

- Treat first while assessing severity. Mark as immediately life-threatening when there is exhaustion, altered consciousness, cyanosis, silent/poorly ventilated chest, bradycardia/hypotension, refractory hypoxemia or impending arrest. PEF at most about 33% of best/predicted supports life-threatening obstruction when the patient can perform it, but inability to perform PEF must not delay treatment or reassure.
- Give controlled oxygen only when hypoxemic; for most adults/adolescents without hypercapnia risk, use a working SpO2 target around 93--95% and avoid hyperoxia, while following the current local/GINA target. Use 88--92% when there is a separate established risk of hypercapnic respiratory failure. Obtain an arterial/venous blood gas for life-threatening features, poor response or suspected ventilatory failure; a normal or rising PaCO2 in a distressed patient is dangerous and may indicate fatigue.
- Give inhaled salbutamol promptly: 4--10 puffs of 100 micrograms through pMDI plus spacer every 20 minutes for the first hour, or 2.5--5 mg by oxygen- or air-driven nebulizer every 20 minutes for three doses when severe, unable to use the spacer or requiring oxygen. Reassess after each cycle. For refractory severe obstruction, a protocolized continuous nebulization (commonly 10--15 mg/h in adults) may be used in a resuscitation/critical-care setting; state drug concentration and nebulizer delivery plan rather than mislabeling it as an IV mL/h infusion.
- Add ipratropium for severe or life-threatening attacks: 0.5 mg nebulized every 20 minutes for three doses, or 4--8 puffs of 20 micrograms via spacer with the initial salbutamol cycles. Continue only according to severity and protocol after the first hour.
- Give systemic corticosteroid early: prednisolone/prednisone 40--50 mg PO once daily for 5--7 days in most adults; if oral treatment is not possible, use an equivalent IV dose according to formulary. Higher IV doses are not routinely superior. Ensure an inhaled-corticosteroid-containing controller and technique/adherence plan before discharge; never discharge an asthma patient on SABA-only treatment.
- For severe obstruction with inadequate initial response, give magnesium sulfate 2 g IV over 20 minutes. Example: 2 g diluted to 100 mL = 20 mg/mL, run at 300 mL/h for 20 minutes; this is a timed intermittent infusion, not continuous therapy. Check hypotension, renal failure and neuromuscular risk; routine repeated magnesium is not established.
- Do not use sedatives, mucolytics, routine antibiotics, routine epinephrine/terbutaline injection or IV aminophylline. Give IM epinephrine when asthma is part of anaphylaxis or angioedema. NIV is not routine in acute asthma and must not delay intubation. If deterioration, exhaustion, reduced consciousness or refractory gas-exchange failure develops, call ICU/anesthesia early and intubate with an experienced operator using a low respiratory rate, long expiratory time, low external PEEP initially, permissive hypercapnia when safe and monitoring for dynamic hyperinflation/hypotension.
- Reassess every 15--30 minutes during the first hour: speech, accessory-muscle use, respiratory rate, air entry, SpO2, HR/BP, PEF/FEV1 when feasible, potassium, glucose and lactate when beta-agonist exposure is high. Rising lactate may be beta-agonist related and must not automatically trigger more bronchodilator or fluid without reassessing airflow and perfusion.
- Admit persistent severe symptoms, oxygen need, low lung function, prior near-fatal asthma, poor social safety or relapse after initial response. ICU for life-threatening features or escalating support. Discharge only after sustained clinical/lung-function improvement, with controller therapy, written action plan, trigger/technique review and follow-up within days.

## copd-exacerbation

- Confirm acute worsening beyond usual variation while actively seeking mimics/precipitants: pneumonia, heart failure, pulmonary embolism, pneumothorax, ACS/arrhythmia, pleural effusion, medication/sedative effect and metabolic disease. Severe dyspnea, confusion, cyanosis, edema, hemodynamic instability or inability to speak/eat requires monitored resuscitation assessment.
- Give controlled oxygen to SpO2 88--92% pending blood gas when COPD or another hypercapnia risk is present. Obtain an early gas in severe illness or oxygen need and repeat about 30--60 minutes after a material oxygen/support change. Do not withhold oxygen from life-threatening hypoxemia, but titrate promptly because excessive oxygen can worsen hypercapnia/acidosis.
- Start short-acting bronchodilators: salbutamol 2.5--5 mg nebulized or 4--8 puffs of 100 micrograms through pMDI/spacer, repeated every 20 minutes for up to three initial cycles according to severity, then space to response. Add ipratropium 0.5 mg nebulized or 4--8 puffs of 20 micrograms during severe initial treatment. Use air-driven nebulization when feasible in CO2 retainers and provide controlled supplemental oxygen separately; monitor HR, rhythm, potassium, tremor and lactate.
- Give prednisone/prednisolone 40 mg PO once daily for 5 days; no taper is usually needed for this short course unless the patient is chronically steroid-dependent or at risk of adrenal suppression. If oral therapy is impossible, use an equivalent IV dose and switch to oral promptly. Avoid routine prolonged or high-dose courses.
- Give antibiotics for purulent sputum with compatible bacterial exacerbation, documented prior pathogenic culture, pneumonia or need for invasive/noninvasive mechanical ventilation; typical duration is 5 days when clinical response is adequate. Choose agent and dose from local resistance, recent antibiotics/cultures, bronchiectasis/Pseudomonas risk, allergy, renal function and pneumonia severity; load `empiric-antibiotics` rather than inventing a universal regimen.
- Use bilevel NIV early for persistent acute or acute-on-chronic hypercapnic respiratory acidosis (generally pH at most 7.35 with elevated PaCO2) despite initial treatment, unless there is an immediate airway indication or contraindication. Load `noninvasive-ventilation`, define failure criteria and repeat gas at about 1 hour. HFNO may improve comfort/oxygenation but is not a substitute for NIV in hypercapnic acidosis. Intubate for arrest, inability to protect the airway, worsening consciousness, refractory hypoxemia/acidosis, shock, secretion burden or failed NIV when consistent with goals of care.
- Do not use methylxanthines routinely. Treat fluids, diuresis and anticoagulation only for the demonstrated phenotype; provide VTE prophylaxis to hospitalized immobile patients unless contraindicated. Continue/restart appropriate long-acting bronchodilators, inhaler technique, smoking treatment and pulmonary-rehabilitation/follow-up planning after stabilization.
- Reassess respiratory rate/effort, mental state, SpO2, gas/pH/PaCO2, hemodynamics, secretion clearance and NIV tolerance frequently. Admit for acidosis/hypercapnia, new oxygen or ventilatory support, serious comorbidity, inadequate home support or failure of ED therapy; higher-acuity placement for severe acidosis, altered consciousness, shock or escalating support.

## life-threatening-hemoptysis

- Define life-threatening hemoptysis by airway obstruction, impaired gas exchange, hemodynamic instability, rapid ongoing bleeding or limited cardiopulmonary reserve, not by a single volume threshold. Confirm blood is from the lower respiratory tract while simultaneously excluding upper-airway and gastrointestinal bleeding; do not delay resuscitation for source certainty.
- Activate anesthesia/airway, respiratory/interventional bronchoscopy, interventional radiology and thoracic surgery early. Sit upright if the bleeding side is unknown and airway control is adequate; once the side is known, place the bleeding lung dependent to protect the better lung. Give high-concentration oxygen for critical hypoxemia, two large-bore IV lines, continuous monitoring, full blood count, coagulation/fibrinogen, renal function, blood gas/lactate and crossmatched blood.
- Stop and reverse clinically important anticoagulant/antiplatelet effect according to agent, timing, renal function and bleeding severity by loading `anticoagulation-reversal`; correct severe thrombocytopenia, hypofibrinogenemia and hypothermia. Do not delay airway/source control while awaiting laboratory normalization.
- Secure the airway early when clearance is failing, oxygenation is deteriorating, bleeding is brisk or transport/procedure is unsafe. Use the most experienced operator, suction immediately available and a large single-lumen endotracheal tube (commonly at least 8.0--8.5 mm internal diameter) to permit therapeutic bronchoscopy. Do not default to a double-lumen tube because narrow lumens obstruct with clot and impede bronchoscopy. Isolate the bleeding side with bronchoscopic mainstem positioning or a bronchial blocker when expertise/equipment permit, and confirm the ventilated lung.
- Use rigid or large-channel flexible bronchoscopy to suction clot, localize bleeding and apply temporary endobronchial control when unstable or the airway is threatened. If stabilized, obtain multidetector CT angiography before definitive embolization to identify bronchial, non-bronchial systemic or pulmonary arterial sources; do not send an unstable patient away from resuscitation solely for imaging.
- Arrange urgent bronchial/non-bronchial systemic artery embolization after stabilization for life-threatening or recurrent hemoptysis when a culprit vessel is present; it is usually first-line definitive control for systemic arterial bleeding. Escalate to thoracic surgery for traumatic or iatrogenic vascular injury, resectable localized disease, pulmonary-artery source, failed/unavailable embolization or recurrent bleeding requiring definitive resection.
- Nebulized tranexamic acid may be considered only as an adjunct/bridge in selected non-catastrophic or temporarily controlled bleeding; a commonly studied regimen is 500 mg in 5 mL nebulized every 8 hours. Evidence in actively life-threatening hemoptysis is insufficient, and it must never delay bronchoscopy, embolization or surgery. Verify formulation, thrombosis/seizure risk and local protocol; do not nebulize blindly through an obstructed airway.
- Reassess airway patency, suctioned/estimated bleeding rate, oxygenation/ventilation, hemodynamics, hemoglobin/coagulation and recurrence continuously. After apparent control, manage the cause (bronchiectasis, TB/infection, malignancy, aspergillosis, vasculitis, pulmonary embolic/vascular lesion) and admit to critical care or a monitored procedural center; early rebleeding requires renewed CTA/bronchoscopy and embolization/surgery review.

## oxygen-therapy-emergency

- Prescribe oxygen to a saturation target, not merely a flow. Use SpO2 94--98% for most acutely ill adults and 88--92% pending blood gas results when COPD or another risk of hypercapnic respiratory failure is present. In cardiac arrest/peri-arrest or immediately life-threatening hypoxemia, give the highest available concentration first and titrate promptly once reliable monitoring is available. Do not give routine oxygen to a normoxemic ACS or stroke patient.
- Treat the following low-flow FiO2 values as rough bedside estimates, not measurements. Actual FiO2 changes with inspiratory demand, respiratory rate, tidal volume, mouth breathing, mask seal and equipment:

| Device | Adult flow | Approximate FiO2 | Operational point |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Standard nasal cannula | 1--6 L/min | about 24--44%; rough rule 24% at 1 L/min then +4 percentage points per additional L/min | Comfortable; permits speech/eating; unreliable FiO2 in tachypnea or high inspiratory demand; dryness/epistaxis possible. |
| Simple face mask | 5--10 L/min | about 35--60% | Never run below 5 L/min because of CO2 rebreathing; variable FiO2, poor tolerance/seal and aspiration risk. |
| Reservoir/non-rebreather mask | 15 L/min initially | high concentration, often about 60--95% with good seal | Pre-fill reservoir and keep it from collapsing during inspiration; bridge for critical illness, not a reason to delay HFNO/NIV/intubation. |
| Venturi mask | Use flow printed on the valve/device | fixed labelled FiO2, commonly 24%, 28%, 35%, 40% or 60% | Prefer when controlled FiO2 matters. Valve colours and required flows can vary by manufacturer; never prescribe by colour alone. Increasing source flow above the stated minimum increases total gas flow, not the labelled FiO2. |
| Heated high-flow nasal oxygen | commonly 20--60 L/min in adults | FiO2 set independently from 0.21 to 1.0 | Heated humidification, better matching of inspiratory demand, dead-space washout and modest variable airway pressure; define failure criteria and do not delay intubation. |

- For a common Venturi system only, typical minimum source flows are 24% at 2--4 L/min, 28% at 4--6 L/min, 35% at 8--12 L/min, 40% at 10--15 L/min and 60% at 12--15 L/min. Verify the exact valve label/package and increase total flow when respiratory demand exceeds device output; changing flow does not convert one labelled valve to another FiO2.
- Reassess within minutes after every device/flow change: SpO2 waveform and target, respiratory rate and effort, mental state, hemodynamics, mask/cannula position, reservoir inflation and oxygen supply. Obtain an early blood gas when severe hypoxemia, suspected hypercapnia, reduced consciousness or escalating support is present; repeat within 30--60 minutes after oxygen changes in patients at risk of CO2 retention or sooner if deteriorating.
- Escalate support rather than repeatedly increasing an inadequate low-flow device. Consider HFNO for hypoxemic failure, NIV for an appropriate ventilatory/cardiogenic indication, and intubation for refractory hypoxemia, exhaustion, worsening consciousness, shock, inability to protect the airway or failed noninvasive support.
- Balance benefit against harm: oxygen reverses hypoxemia but excessive exposure can worsen hypercapnia in susceptible patients, cause absorption atelectasis and oxidative lung injury, and mask deterioration. Interfaces can cause dryness, pressure injury, claustrophobia and aspiration risk. Oxygen supports combustion: prohibit smoking/flames and keep oils/grease away from equipment.
- Record target range, device, flow or set FiO2, SpO2 response and escalation plan. Flow in L/min is not interchangeable with FiO2, and FiO2 from low-flow devices must not be used as an exact denominator for P/F calculations.

## high-flow-nasal-oxygen

- Use heated humidified high-flow nasal oxygen primarily for acute hypoxemic respiratory failure when conventional oxygen is inadequate and no immediate indication for intubation exists. HFNO does not correct severe ventilatory failure reliably and must not replace bilevel NIV for a COPD exacerbation with acute respiratory acidosis when NIV is appropriate.
- In adults, select an interface sized to avoid complete nare occlusion, commonly start flow around 40--60 L/min according to inspiratory demand and tolerance, and set FiO2 independently to the syndrome-specific SpO2 target. Optimize the cause, positioning, secretion clearance and comfort; do not treat the device as definitive therapy.
- Calculate ROX only from a reliable SpO2, set HFNO FiO2 as a fraction, and simultaneous respiratory rate: `ROX = (SpO2 / FiO2) / respiratory rate`. Use `scripts/respiratory_support.py` for serial calculations. Record baseline and repeat early and at approximately 2, 6 and 12 hours when therapy continues, but reassess clinically within minutes after initiation or any deterioration.
- Treat published ROX cutoffs as context-limited signals from adults with pneumonia, not universal intubation thresholds. In the original validation cohort, ROX at least 4.88 at 2, 6 or 12 hours was associated with lower intubation risk; ROX below 2.85 at 2 hours, 3.47 at 6 hours or 3.85 at 12 hours signaled higher failure risk. The direction of change and the bedside examination matter more than a single borderline value.
- Define failure before starting. Escalate immediately for refractory or worsening hypoxemia, increasing respiratory effort or fatigue, shock, worsening mental state, inability to protect the airway, dangerous acidosis/hypercapnia, or another immediate airway indication. A reassuring ROX must never overrule these findings or delay intubation.
- Monitor continuous SpO2 and frequent respiratory rate, work of breathing, mental state and hemodynamics; obtain blood gas and imaging/POCUS according to physiology. Document flow, FiO2, target, ROX trend, response, ceiling of care and named escalation plan.

## noninvasive-ventilation

- Match NIV to a supported syndrome. Recommend bilevel NIV for COPD exacerbation with acute or acute-on-chronic respiratory acidosis, generally pH at most 7.35 with elevated PaCO2 despite initial medical therapy. Recommend CPAP or bilevel NIV for acute cardiogenic pulmonary edema without shock or another immediate airway indication. NIV is not routine treatment for acute asthma, and de-novo hypoxemic failure requires close critical-care surveillance because failure can be harmful if intubation is delayed.
- Before starting, document reversibility, escalation/ceiling plan and contraindications. Do not use NIV as a delaying maneuver in respiratory arrest, inability to protect the airway, uncontrolled vomiting, fixed upper-airway obstruction, undrained pneumothorax, severe facial injury/burn preventing an interface, or rapidly worsening shock. Agitation, secretions, reduced consciousness and severe acidosis increase failure risk and require senior critical-care assessment rather than an automatic exclusion or automatic trial.
- For acute hypercapnic failure, use a protocol-compatible ventilator and appropriate vented/non-vented circuit. A common S/T reference start is IPAP 10--15 and EPAP 4 cmH2O with backup rate 12--16/min; increase IPAP over about 10--30 minutes toward effective chest expansion/ventilation, commonly 20--30 cmH2O, while checking leak, synchrony, tidal volume and tolerance. Do not exceed local device/interface limits; IPAP above 30 or EPAP above 8 requires expert/local-protocol review.
- If PaCO2 remains high with inadequate ventilation, correct leak/synchrony and increase pressure support by raising IPAP. If hypoxemia persists, verify cause and interface, then titrate FiO2 and/or EPAP while preserving adequate pressure support. For acute cardiogenic pulmonary edema, a common CPAP reference range is 5--10 cmH2O; use bilevel when ventilatory assistance is also required. These are starting references, not patient-independent prescriptions.
- Target SpO2 88--92% in acute hypercapnic respiratory failure unless a documented individualized target applies. Use continuous SpO2 and cardiac monitoring initially; reassess symptoms, respiratory rate/effort, consciousness, BP, synchrony, leak and gas exchange. Repeat gas approximately 1 hour after initiation and after material setting changes, and again around 4 hours when continuing, or sooner with deterioration.
- Treat falling pH, failure of PaCO2 or work of breathing to improve, persistent inability to maintain target oxygenation, respiratory rate persistently above about 25/min, new confusion, intolerance or hemodynamic deterioration as urgent failure signals. pH below 7.25 despite optimized NIV demands immediate senior/ICU review, but no single number replaces the full intubation decision. Prepare invasive ventilation early when escalation is appropriate.

## tension-pneumothorax

- Suspect tension physiology when acute dyspnea or pleuritic chest pain is accompanied by shock, severe respiratory compromise or rapid deterioration, especially with asymmetric or absent breath sounds. A relatively normal heart rate, absent tracheal deviation or absent neck-vein distension does not establish stability or exclude the diagnosis.
- Treat this as a clinical time-critical diagnosis. Activate resuscitation support, provide monitored oxygen appropriate to hypoxemia, obtain IV/IO access and prepare immediate pleural decompression. Do not send an unstable patient for CT, and do not wait for chest radiography when the clinical probability of tension pneumothorax is high.
- Use lung POCUS only when it is immediately available and does not delay decompression. Absent sliding and B-lines with a compatible pattern support pneumothorax but are not specific; a lung point supports the diagnosis when seen but may be absent in a very large pneumothorax. A technically limited or negative scan does not overrule worsening obstructive-shock physiology.
- Select needle/catheter decompression or open/finger thoracostomy according to patient context, clinician skill, equipment and current local resuscitation protocol. State the intended anatomical site and device before acting, avoid the inferior rib neurovascular bundle, and have an immediate failure/rescue plan. Do not present one technique or site as universally preferred across spontaneous, traumatic and peri-arrest settings.
- Decompression is not the endpoint: reassess blood pressure, oxygenation, work of breathing and bilateral air entry immediately; confirm pleural access/success by clinical response and available imaging, then arrange definitive intercostal drainage and specialist/critical-care involvement. If there is no prompt improvement, check device failure or malposition and reassess for massive PE, tamponade, hemorrhage, dynamic hyperinflation and other causes of shock.
- Use cautious, reassessed circulatory support only as a bridge to source control. Fluids or vasopressors must not delay decompression or be treated as definitive therapy for obstructive shock.

## dermatology-severe

- Look for mucosal involvement, skin pain, blistering, purpura, necrosis, systemic toxicity, immunosuppression and drug timing.
- Threats include SJS/TEN, DRESS, necrotizing infection, meningococcemia, purpura fulminans and severe drug reaction.
- Stop suspected culprit drugs, stabilize temperature/fluids/analgesia, avoid traumatic skin handling and obtain early dermatology/burn/critical-care input. Verify syndrome-specific steroids or immunomodulators before use.

## soft-tissue-infections

- Distinguish uncomplicated cellulitis/abscess from necrotizing infection, deep-space infection, pyomyositis and device-related infection.
- Pain out of proportion, toxicity, bullae, crepitus, anesthesia or rapid progression requires immediate surgical consultation; imaging must not delay exploration when suspicion is high.
- Drain drainable collections and select antibiotics by severity, anatomy, host factors and local resistance. Mark progression, reassess perfusion and admit systemic, high-risk or function-threatening disease.

## emergency-infectious-diseases

- Define syndrome, exposure/travel, immune status, isolation requirement, organ dysfunction and need for public-health notification.
- Obtain targeted microbiology while treating shock, meningitis, severe malaria and other time-critical syndromes immediately.
- Use local epidemiology and current public-health guidance for testing, isolation and therapy. Reassess diagnosis daily and stop or narrow unnecessary empiric treatment.
