# Disposition and cross-cutting modules

## observation-discharge

- Adult observation is active, time-limited evaluation/treatment for a defined unresolved clinical question, not a place to defer necessary admission or source control. Define entry criteria, required serial tests/exams, treatment endpoints, reassessment times and the syndrome/local unit's maximum safe duration. Assign the responsible clinician and nursing team, with explicit handover and escalation criteria; do not impose one observation time for all conditions.
- Match the plan to actual staffing, equipment and rescue capability. Reassess symptoms, vital signs, consciousness, examination, function and treatment response at the required interval and after a material change; abnormal trends require action even if a score is low. Deterioration, unresolved high-risk diagnosis, escalating support needs or inability to complete the plan safely triggers senior reassessment, inpatient care or transfer.
- Apply the condition-specific discharge pathway, including required serial ECG/laboratory/imaging or post-treatment observation where indicated. A normal single test, symptom improvement, completion of a clock interval or a risk score alone is insufficient for discharge. Check oral intake/medication use, mobility, pain control, post-sedation recovery and relevant pregnancy/frailty/safeguarding needs rather than relying on stable BP alone.
- Review available results and reconcile preliminary versus final interpretations before disposition. If a test can safely remain pending, name the clinician/service responsible for reviewing it, the expected timeframe, the contact method, and the action/escalation plan for a significant result. Verify usable contact details and communicate the plan to the patient and next team; electronic release or 'no news means normal' is not closed-loop follow-up. A result essential to an immediate safe decision must not be left unresolved merely because follow-up is planned.
- Provide a clear working diagnosis and uncertainty, reconciled medication changes/duration and precautions, condition-specific deterioration warnings, where/how urgently to return and concrete follow-up arrangements. Use the patient's language, interpreter/accessibility support and teach-back when possible. Check understanding, decision-making capacity, transport, caregiver support and ability to carry out the plan; address barriers before discharge.
- For admission/transfer, communicate the reason, current status, recent interventions, pending tests, monitoring/rescue needs and named receiving responsibility. Document shared decisions or informed refusal/capacity and the safety plan. Local observation capacity, specialist response times and result-callback systems must be confirmed operationally; this reference review does not certify their existence.

## hematology-oncology

- Adult recognition/escalation framework: threats include neutropenic sepsis, leukostasis, tumor lysis syndrome (TLS), hyperviscosity, spinal cord compression, malignant hypercalcemia and major bleeding/thrombosis. Record cancer/transplant diagnosis, recent systemic therapy, immunosuppression, prophylaxis, central access and treating-team details. Assess ABCDE, organ dysfunction, glucose and neurological status; minimal local signs or absent fever do not establish safety.
- An unwell patient receiving anticancer treatment may have neutropenic sepsis. Obtain cultures, blood count/differential, renal/liver function, lactate and source-directed tests promptly, without waiting for the neutrophil count or culture results to start urgently indicated empirical antibiotics. Use `sepsis-shock` and `empiric-antibiotics`, with antipseudomonal coverage chosen for allergy, renal function, prior resistant organisms and local epidemiology. Do not discharge from a low-risk score alone; outpatient care requires a formal oncology risk assessment and reliable treatment/review pathway.
- Suspect spontaneous or treatment-related TLS with high disease burden, renal dysfunction or evolving potassium, phosphate, calcium, urate and creatinine abnormalities. Arrange ECG, serial biochemical monitoring, strict fluid balance and immediate hematology/nephrology involvement; hydration must account for renal/cardiac tolerance. Prevention and established TLS require different specialist regimens. Select urate-lowering therapy promptly by risk and product information; rasburicase is contraindicated in G6PD deficiency and can falsely lower urate in mishandled samples. Follow the laboratory's specific sample-handling instructions. Do not routinely correct asymptomatic TLS-associated hypocalcemia; symptomatic or ECG-threatening disturbances need the relevant emergency protocol. Escalate refractory electrolyte/volume or renal complications for renal replacement assessment.
- New respiratory or neurological compromise with suspected acute leukemia/hyperleukocytosis, symptomatic hyperviscosity, major bleeding or coagulopathy requires immediate hematology/critical-care discussion and syndrome-specific treatment; do not wait for complete disease classification. A cell count alone cannot determine leukostasis or a universal cytoreduction/apheresis plan.
- Route new spinal pain/weakness/sphincter dysfunction to `acute-spinal-cord-compression`, significant hypercalcemia to `calcium-magnesium-emergencies`, and bleeding/thrombosis to the corresponding hemorrhage, reversal or thromboembolism module. These conditions must not wait for a routine outpatient cancer appointment.
- Reassess infection, perfusion, neurological function, renal/electrolyte trends and treatment toxicity. Admit/escalate organ dysfunction, suspected TLS/leukostasis, uncontrolled symptoms or inability to provide reliable monitoring. This framework does not authorize chemotherapy, apheresis or an unverified local drug preparation; document specialist decisions and transfer/handover needs.

## ophthalmology-emergencies

- Adult initial framework: establish onset/last known normal vision, pain, trauma/chemical exposure, contact lenses, recent eye surgery/injection and systemic symptoms. Assess visual acuity in each eye when feasible, pupils/RAPD, fields, motility and relevant anterior/posterior findings without delaying immediate rescue. A photograph or a limited examination does not replace the required clinical eye assessment; use pediatric-specific advice for children.
- Chemical exposure: begin copious irrigation immediately, before detailed history, visual testing or photography; do not wait for a preferred solution if clean water is immediately available. Remove ongoing contamination/contact lenses or retained particles when safely feasible, continue irrigation and reassess ocular-surface pH to an appropriate physiological endpoint with specialist guidance. Do not attempt chemical neutralization with an opposite-pH substance. Suspected simultaneous open globe needs immediate ophthalmology-led trauma/decontamination decisions; avoid pressure or forced irrigation. Arrange urgent ophthalmology review according to injury severity; no fixed volume alone proves adequate decontamination.
- Suspected open globe/penetrating injury: protect with a rigid shield without pressure, avoid manipulation, tonometry and pressure-producing ocular ultrasound, and do not remove an embedded object. Give appropriate systemic symptom control, keep ready for urgent operative assessment and contact ophthalmology immediately; further imaging/antimicrobial/tetanus decisions follow the trauma and eye-specialist protocol. A small wound or retained vision does not exclude serious penetration.
- Sudden painless monocular visual loss, a new field curtain or new flashes/floaters with visual disturbance requires urgent retinal/vascular assessment, not routine follow-up. Suspected retinal arterial occlusion activates immediate ophthalmology and stroke assessment; record last known normal and assess associated neurological signs. Reperfusion decisions belong to the current stroke/eye team and evidence, not an automatic ocular massage, paracentesis or empiric thrombolysis order from this framework.
- A painful red eye with reduced vision, corneal change, headache/nausea or abnormal pupil may be acute angle closure or another sight-threatening disorder. Measure IOP only when open globe is not suspected and obtain urgent ophthalmology assessment. Mechanism matters: topiramate-associated or other secondary angle closure is not automatically treated like pupillary block; do not give a universal pilocarpine regimen. Verify specific pressure-lowering drugs, contraindications and actual preparation with the specialist/product protocol.
- Suspect endophthalmitis with a painful red eye and reduced vision after surgery/injection, and orbital infection with proptosis, painful/restricted movements, visual impairment or systemic illness. Escalate immediately for specialist imaging, antimicrobials and source control as indicated; do not treat these as uncomplicated conjunctivitis. New visual loss with symptoms suggesting giant-cell arteritis also needs an immediate specialist/systemic treatment pathway, without waiting for routine eye follow-up.
- Reassess vision, pain, pupils and physiology after initial treatment. Discharge only after dangerous causes have been addressed and required specialist assessment completed, with an appropriate treatment plan, explicit deterioration advice and reliable follow-up. Unresolved loss of vision, suspected open globe, chemical injury, acute angle closure, retinal vascular/detachment concern or orbital/intraocular infection requires urgent specialist care/admission/transfer according to the actual pathway; on-site ophthalmology availability is not presumed.

## ent-emergencies

- Adult recognition/escalation framework: prioritize airway compromise, major hemorrhage, deep-neck infection/supraglottitis, mastoid/orbital or intracranial spread, sudden sensorineural hearing loss and hazardous foreign bodies. Assess ABCDE and consider children separately; do not apply adult examination, sedation or drug pathways automatically to a child.
- Severe throat pain with drooling, voice change, stridor, respiratory distress, trismus or systemic illness warrants urgent senior ENT/anesthesia assessment. A normal visible oropharynx does not exclude supraglottitis. Avoid forced/distressing examination or transfer to imaging before a threatened airway is secured; use `airway-rsi` with a controlled difficult-airway/rescue plan. Bedside intervention and transfer depend on actual expertise and resources, not assumed on-site ENT availability.
- Suspected deep-neck abscess, Ludwig angina, Lemierre syndrome or spreading infection needs urgent ENT/maxillofacial and sepsis assessment. Obtain contrast imaging when clinically safe and it will guide care; start indicated IV antibiotics through `empiric-antibiotics` and arrange drainage/source control when required. Imaging and cultures must not delay airway stabilization or sepsis treatment.
- For epistaxis, assess airway, circulation, ongoing blood loss and anticoagulants; start appropriate local first-line measures, including sustained soft-nose compression and anatomy-directed hemostasis/packing by trained staff. Persistent, recurrent or suspected posterior bleeding requires ENT escalation and possible operative/interventional control. In non-life-threatening bleeding, do not automatically stop/reverse anticoagulation before local first-line treatment; life-threatening bleeding uses `anticoagulation-reversal` and the hemorrhage pathway with specialist coordination.
- Sudden hearing loss not explained by external/middle-ear disease needs time-critical ENT assessment. NICE NG98: onset over 3 days or less within the past 30 days requires review within 24 hours. New focal neurological findings also require the appropriate stroke pathway. Do not assume wax/otitis without assessment or defer to routine audiology.
- Button batteries, caustic material, airway/esophageal foreign bodies or objects threatening tissue need immediate appropriate specialty advice and controlled removal, not repeated blind attempts. Arrange urgent assessment for mastoid swelling, orbital signs, facial weakness or neurological deterioration. Discharge only after airway/bleeding stability, adequate swallowing/hydration, a safe symptom plan and explicit follow-up/return instructions; unresolved red flags need observation, admission or transfer.

## environmental-emergencies

- Adult initial framework: stop exposure while protecting rescuers, assess ABCDE, trauma, glucose, ECG, electrolytes and organ injury, and obtain a reliable core temperature when relevant. Document exposure duration, exertion, immersion, medications/toxins and treatment already given; use pediatric-specific pathways for children.
- Heat stroke is suspected with heat/exertion exposure and CNS dysfunction; do not postpone cooling for a perfect temperature reading or a rigid diagnostic cutoff. Start rapid active cooling while supporting airway/perfusion. Cold-water immersion is effective when feasible with airway protection and continuous supervision; adapt the method to patient/context. Monitor core temperature and prevent overshoot. Antipyretics do not treat heat stroke. Distinguish exercise-associated hyponatremia, intoxication and malignant hyperthermia; their treatments differ. Suspected heat stroke needs monitored assessment for renal, hepatic, coagulation and muscle injury.
- In hypothermia, remove wet exposure, insulate, handle gently and use severity-appropriate active rewarming with core-temperature/ECG monitoring. For an unconscious hypothermic patient, assess signs of life for up to 1 minute. Use the 2025 temperature-modified resuscitation protocol rather than an unchanged normothermic drug/shock schedule; discuss severe instability or arrest early with an extracorporeal-rewarming center. Availability and transport feasibility must be confirmed, not assumed.
- Drowning prioritizes safe rescue, ventilation and oxygenation. For adult resuscitation use the ERC/RCUK 2025 sequence, beginning with 5 rescue breaths then standard CPR when indicated. Suspected spinal injury precautions must not delay essential extrication/resuscitation. Assess associated hypothermia and evolving respiratory failure; route ongoing respiratory support to `acute-respiratory-failure` and monitor symptomatic patients rather than clearing them from one normal initial finding.
- Use `toxicology`, `anaphylaxis`, `major-burns` and `smoke-inhalation-toxic-injury` for relevant exposures; electrical injury, decompression/altitude illness and bites/envenomation need their own expert assessment and disposition. Do not generalize one cooling, rewarming, antidote or hyperbaric plan to all environmental emergencies.
- Reassess mental state, temperature, oxygenation, hemodynamics, urine output and laboratory trends. Escalate persistent neurological/respiratory abnormalities, shock or organ injury; discharge only after syndrome-appropriate observation/recovery, reliable follow-up and clear deterioration warnings. This review covers the initial environmental framework, not every specialist exposure-treatment regimen.

## ecg-pocus-integration

- Start from the clinical question and pretest probability; record rhythm/rate/intervals/ischemia on ECG and focused cardiac/lung/vascular findings on POCUS.
- Reconcile discordant data rather than allowing one test to overrule the patient. Repeat studies after intervention or clinical change.
- State technical limitations and alternatives. Normal or limited studies do not exclude ACS, PE, aortic disease, early tamponade or other high-risk syndromes.

## pocus

- Define a focused question, obtain standard views, save images and document operator/technical limitations.
- Integrate findings with physiology and other testing; use serial POCUS for response and evolving disease where appropriate.
- Do not convert qualitative findings into unsupported volume status or diagnostic certainty. A single still frame cannot establish motion-dependent signs such as lung sliding; a negative or technically limited study cannot exclude a high-risk syndrome outside the validated scope of that examination.
- Record the operator, indication, views, adequacy, findings, interpretation and resulting action. Seek comprehensive imaging or expert review when the decision exceeds focused ultrasound capability.
